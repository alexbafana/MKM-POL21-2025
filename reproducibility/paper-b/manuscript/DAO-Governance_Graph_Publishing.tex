\begin{abstract}
Institutional knowledge graphs evolve through validation,
publication, revision, and retirement. Governing these changes
requires clear responsibility, explicit authorization, and
evidence that approved operations produce the intended results.
Knowledge graph engineering and decentralized autonomous
organization (DAO) modeling provide complementary foundations,
but their combination requires adaptation to institutional
governance.
Following design science research, we derive 36 requirements
from an institutional employment-information case and the
literature. We specialize the DAO Modeling Method (DAOMod),
using the DAO Modeling Language (DAO-ML) to represent
organizational structures and permissions, and Extending
Trusted DApp Modeling (ET-DM) to represent decision processes
and execution. The design assigns authority over specific
versions of knowledge graphs and related assets, connects
collective decisions to authorized operations and execution
records, and specifies safeguards for accountability,
provenance, reversibility, compliance, and protection against
the concentration or misuse of governance power.
We assess implementation support by comparing the requirements
with smart-contract code and test results. The baseline
prototype directly exercises two requirements, partially supports
sixteen, and does not implement eighteen. A supplemental local
test connects committee approval to verified graph publication.
A further proof of concept uses selected elements of the worked
models to configure and execute a WoodHive publication process,
linking the decision, content checks, and receipt to the model.
Both demonstrations assume trusted validation and publication
components. The results
demonstrate selected parts of the design; its effectiveness
in institutional practice and its legal adequacy require
further evaluation.
The contribution includes an integrated ontology, worked
models, proposed visual notation, and mappings between model
elements and requirements. Automated checks verify that the
model files follow the checked schemas and that their
requirement references resolve. %Independent expert review and reproduction remain outstanding. The usability of the notation and the effectiveness of the complete governance design remain unevaluated.
\end{abstract}

A knowledge graph (KG) represents entities and their relationships
in a machine-readable form \cite{Hogan2021KnowledgeGraphs}.
Organizations can use these representations to support decisions,
coordinate activities, and provide information services.
For example, Serderidis et al. represent government decisions
and acts as knowledge graphs \cite{serderidis2024d2kg}.
The business value of such information depends on whether users
can judge it fit for their purpose. They need to know which
version is approved, where its information comes from, who is
responsible for changes, and how errors are corrected.
Transparency and traceability are therefore important concerns
in knowledge-driven data ecosystems
\cite{geisler2021transparency}.

Governance addresses these concerns by assigning responsibility
and decision rights throughout a graph's validation, publication,
revision, and retirement. Clear approval procedures and records
of changes can help organizations reduce repeated checking,
investigate errors, and coordinate the release of information.
These are potential benefits of governance; this study does
not measure their financial or operational impact.
We refer to the identifiable graphs and related resources
subject to these controls as \emph{semantic assets}.

The need for collective governance becomes particularly clear
when several organizations contribute to or rely on a shared
graph, but none is accepted as its sole decision-maker.
They need agreed procedures for approving changes and evidence
that the approved operations are carried out. Decentralized
autonomous organizations (DAOs) offer mechanisms for expressing
collective decision rules and connecting decisions to execution
\cite{FaqirRhazoui2020DAOOverview,Hassan2021DAO}.
This motivates our investigation of DAO-based knowledge graph
governance. The rationale concerns shared authority across
participants; a graph under a single organization's control
may instead be adequately governed through conventional
approval workflows and audit records.

Knowledge graph research provides several foundations for managing
changes to graph content, covering modeling, querying, validation,
construction, and publication \cite{Hogan2021KnowledgeGraphs}.
These foundations answer different technical questions: SHACL checks
whether data satisfies declared constraints \cite{Knublauch2017SHACL},
PROV describes its provenance \cite{Moreau2013PROV,Moreau2015Rationale},
and Dibowski's change-tracking approach supports recovery of earlier
versions \cite{Dibowski2024TraceabilityKG}. Geisler et al. place
integration, updates, querying, and provenance within a recurring
knowledge graph lifecycle \cite{Geisler2025KGLifecycle}.
Their relevance to governance becomes clearer when graphs support
institutional activities: KG.gov proposes their use in AI data governance
\cite{MeronoPenuela2025KGgov}, while Zhang et al. examine human oversight
and trustworthy construction \cite{Zhang2025TrustworthyKG}.
Together, these approaches motivate examining how technical evidence
is connected to authority over a graph's use and modification.

DAO research provides mechanisms for making collective decisions and
connecting them to execution through blockchain infrastructure
\cite{FaqirRhazoui2020DAOOverview,Hassan2021DAO}. Whether those
mechanisms support a viable organization also depends on accountability,
decision models, and incentives, as examined by Rikken et al.
\cite{rikken2023governance}, and on the organizational context and
members' perspectives investigated by Augustin et al.
\cite{augustin2023understanding}. For institutional knowledge graphs,
these concerns become concrete questions: who may approve a particular
version, what operation that approval permits, and what evidence shows
that the operation is carried out. Our specialization addresses those
connections rather than assuming that a voting mechanism establishes them.

Evidence about data use must explain both how information is managed
and why its claims deserve consideration. Papadopoulou et al. address
the planning aspect by linking machine-actionable data management plans
to the OpenAIRE Research Graph \cite{papadopoulou2023argos}.
ProVe examines support for graph claims in referenced source texts
\cite{amaral2022prove}, while Piscopo et al. assess the relevance and
authoritativeness of Wikidata references
\cite{Piscopo2017WikidataProvenance}. These studies provide context for
our use of evidence, but our question concerns a further connection:
how collective authorization is tied to a specific lifecycle operation
and an observable result.

The presence of these mechanisms does not establish that authority
is effectively distributed. Participation, concentration of
control, and governance vulnerabilities remain design concerns
\cite{Han2025DAOReview}. Governance can also span forums,
off-chain polling, nested organizations, and cross-chain
execution, making decisions and responsibilities difficult
to trace \cite{Weidener2026Metagovernance}. Institutional use
therefore requires explicit connections between decision rights,
collective outcomes, and execution.

Model-driven DAO engineering supplies reusable modeling
constructs. DAO-ML represents roles, committees, governance
areas, permissions, and organizational relations
\cite{Avanzo2024DAOML}. Extending Trusted DApp Modeling
(ET-DM), where DApp denotes a decentralized application,
extends T-DM with token-economy modeling
\cite{Avanzo2025ETDM}. Avanzo's thesis describes its use
for the Coordination and Execution layers, including
smart-contract primitives and on-chain/off-chain functions
\cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}.

The 2025 DAOMod article develops an ontology, modeling
language, and method focused on organizational structures
\cite{Avanzo2025DAOMod}. Avanzo's thesis presents the broader
integration of DAO-ML and ET-DM across Organization,
Coordination, and Execution \cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}.
Related work implements DAO-ML-to-Solidity transformation
\cite{Avanzo2025DAOMLtoSolidity} and develops token-economy
and voting-protocol specification
\cite{Avanzo2026DAOModCoordination}. We use these foundations
to develop the institutional knowledge graph specialization.

The research gap lies in connecting these capabilities for
institutional semantic assets. This requires identifying the
governed graph version, assigning lifecycle-specific authority,
binding a collective decision to an exact operation, and
recording evidence of its result. It also requires safeguards
for correction, compliance, accountable mandates, and
\emph{governance capture}, in which one actor or aligned group
can determine or obstruct collective outcomes.

We address this gap through the following main research
question (MRQ):
\emph{How can DAOMod be specialized to regulate institutional
knowledge graph lifecycles through decentralized governance
and authorization while accommodating domain-specific
governance constraints?}

Three sub-research questions organize the design:

\emph{Research question 1 (RQ1): What adaptations to the DAO-ML
Organization layer are required to represent semantic assets,
lifecycle roles, permissions, and decision rights in
institutional knowledge graphs?}

\emph{Research question 2 (RQ2): What adaptations to the ET-DM
Coordination and Execution layers are required to bind collective
governance decisions to knowledge graph lifecycle transitions
across on-chain and off-chain components?}

\emph{Research question 3 (RQ3): What institutional safeguards
and constraints must extend DAOMod to support accountability,
provenance, reversibility, compliance, and resistance to
governance capture?}

Following design science research \cite{hevner2004design},
we derive 36 requirements from an institutional employment
case and the literature. We distinguish inherited modeling
constructs, adaptations that assign knowledge graph lifecycle
meanings, and newly introduced extensions. The resulting
design separates technical eligibility, institutional
authorization, and execution confirmation.

The contribution makes this specialization inspectable through a
self-contained integrated ontology, linked DAO-ML and ET-DM worked
models, and proposed visual notation. Separate mappings connect all
36 requirements to model representations and implementation evidence,
so representing a requirement is not confused with executing it.
Schema, graph-preservation, binding, and notation checks assess the
consistency of those representations. The baseline assessment then
identifies two requirements with directly exercised support, sixteen
with partial support, and eighteen without a corresponding implementation.
To investigate the missing connection between approval and publication,
a separate supplemental episode follows an exact committee decision
through local RDF publication, content read-back verification, and an
attributable receipt. Its trusted-validator and trusted-adapter
assumptions limit this result to bounded feasibility evidence.
A further model-linked proof of concept tests whether selected
ontology and model additions can configure an executable WoodHive
publication process. It supplies clause-level feasibility evidence,
without reclassifying the historical baseline requirements.

\section{Preliminaries}
\label{sec:preliminaries}

Deriving governance requirements requires a clear account of
what changes, who is responsible, and what evidence supports
each decision. In an AI-assisted knowledge graph pipeline,
a revision can originate in source data, processing configurations,
models, or validation rules. These changes must be distinguishable
before authority over them can be specified.

Section~\ref{sec:runningcase} describes the institutional running case,
its actors, semantic assets, and lifecycle concerns.
Section~\ref{sec:background} defines the concepts used to
represent those concerns. Section~\ref{sec:derivation_protocol}
explains how we translate them into requirements and check
their traceability and coverage.

\subsection{Running Case}
\label{sec:runningcase}

To illustrate the pipeline, we use a hypothetical company
called WoodHive. A hypothetical news article reports that
the company creates 120 jobs in Pärnu, Estonia. This invented
example provides a consistent basis for explaining graph
construction and governance throughout the paper; it does
not describe a verified employment event.

The running case concerns a pipeline that extracts employment
events from Estonian news articles and represents them as
Resource Description Framework (RDF) data. RDF supplies the
representation model \cite{Cyganiak2014RDFConcepts}; the
employment pipeline is the case description used by this study.
Articles from Eesti Rahvusringhääling (ERR; Estonian Public
Broadcasting) provide its source material. Employment events
are linked to the Estonian Classification of Economic Activities
(EMTAK), associated with provenance, and validated for downstream
policy analytics. DAO-based authorization is the proposed
intervention, rather than an existing deployment in this pipeline.

The illustrative article passes through a sequence that makes possible
sources of change visible. Comma-separated values (CSV) ingestion and
JavaScript Object Notation (JSON) transformation prepare the source
records for natural language processing (NLP). Lemma and keyword
extraction and named-entity recognition (NER) identify relevant text
and entities, after which large language model (LLM) classification
and EMTAK alignment assign event and economic-activity categories.
Provenance capture and processing-trajectory records document these
steps before RDF generation and validation produce a candidate graph.
This sequence supplies the governance questions used below; the
WoodHive event remains hypothetical.

Language-specific transformer models such as EstBERT support
Estonian-language analysis, while embedding-based techniques
such as KeyBERT support keyword identification
\cite{Tanvir2021EstBERT,Grootendorst2020KeyBERT}.
The illustrative RDF output represents the employment event
(\texttt{job\_gain}, \texttt{jobCount = 120}), relevant entities,
classification links, and references to its processing history.

Figure~\ref{fig:runningcase_pipeline} separates semantic
processing from the proposed governance intervention.
The upper lane produces the candidate information.
The lower lane associates it with provenance and integrity
evidence, checks technical constraints, and introduces
collective authorization before publication. Validation
establishes whether a candidate satisfies declared checks;
authorization determines whether the institution permits
the proposed lifecycle transition.

\paragraph{Institutional Actors and Responsibilities.}

Table~\ref{tab:runningcase_actors} identifies the actors,
their responsibilities, and the risks associated with
uncontrolled changes. Validators check candidate graphs using SHACL constraints
\cite{Knublauch2017SHACL} and queries written in SPARQL,
a query language for RDF graphs \cite{Harris2013SPARQL}.
Institutional approvers decide whether publication or revision
is authorized, while auditors examine the supporting records.

A person may perform several roles, but the responsibilities
of each role remain distinct. For example, changing an
information-extraction model, a classification rule, or the
selection of source data may change the resulting knowledge
graph even though it remains valid RDF. Technical validity
alone therefore does not establish whether the change is
authorized or who is accountable for it. The case model
addresses these questions separately: what checks the graph
must pass, who may approve the change, and who is accountable
for the decision and its execution.

\paragraph{Semantic Assets and Evidence.}

The running case divides the knowledge graph into identifiable RDF
partitions so that a change to one part can be versioned and governed
separately. The WoodHive partition brings together the source article,
the organization and location entities, and the statements describing
the employment event. Links to EMTAK economic-activity categories then
allow that event to be grouped and analyzed by industry. The examples
use \texttt{ex:} for illustrative resources and \texttt{emp:} for
employment-related concepts and relationships; both are namespace
abbreviations chosen for the example.

PROV-O supplies relations such as \texttt{prov:wasGeneratedBy}
and temporal information for describing provenance
\cite{Moreau2013PROV,Lebo2013PROVO}.
The running case also requires records of how each graph
version is produced and checked. These records identify the
versions of the software models used, the processing steps
and their inputs and outputs, and the results of validation.
A content hash provides a digital fingerprint of a graph
version, allowing later checks for changes to its content.

Named graphs and records of changes to individual RDF
statements provide foundations for associating graph content
with its history
\cite{Carroll2005NamedGraphs,Dibowski2024TraceabilityKG}.
Our design additionally requires cryptographic checks of
content integrity. PROV can describe the graph's origins and
processing history, but recording that information does not
itself verify that the content remains unchanged.

A reliable hash comparison requires a defined procedure.
The RDF content must first be converted into a standardized
form, called canonicalization, and then processed using a
specified hashing algorithm. Otherwise, different
representations of the same graph may produce different
hashes.

\paragraph{Lifecycle Events.}

The case includes the following events:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Initial publication:
    a newly generated candidate becomes available as an
    authoritative graph version.

    \item Model update:
    a change to an NLP, NER, or LLM configuration can alter
    subsequent outputs.

    \item Correction:
    an employment classification or EMTAK mapping is revised.

    \item Reprocessing:
    an RDF partition is regenerated using a changed pipeline
    or model.

    \item Supersession:
    a new version replaces the active version while its
    predecessor remains traceable.

    \item Deprecation:
    a version is withdrawn from active authoritative use
    without deletion.

    \item Restoration:
    an authorized corrective transition returns a retained
    version to active use while preserving the intervening
    history.

    \item Archival:
    a version is retained under the applicable preservation
    and access rules.
\end{itemize}

In the running-case example, changing an article's classification
to \texttt{job\_gain} changes the information used in employment
analysis. We therefore require each governed change to identify
its responsible actors, supporting evidence, and authority.

\paragraph{Governance Concerns.}

The running case provides five principal concerns for requirement
derivation. The following statements express the requirements
we adopt for this case, rather than obligations established
by the cited standards or DAO studies:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Multi-stakeholder authorization:}
    engineers, model maintainers, and institutional authorities
    have distinct responsibilities in accepting or rejecting
    candidate outputs.

    \item \emph{Cross-organizational reliance:}
    institutions need evidence of source authenticity,
    classification alignment, and the interpretation of
    probabilistic outputs.

    \item \emph{Provenance integrity:}
    published versions, and individual claims where required,
    remain linked to sources, processing runs, model versions,
    and integrity evidence. PROV supplies representation
    constructs for entities, activities, agents, and derivation
    \cite{Moreau2013PROV,Lebo2013PROVO}.

    \item \emph{Reproducibility:}
    processing records support inspection and qualified
    re-execution. Identical outputs additionally require
    preservation of sources, models, parameters, and dependencies
    and deterministic execution. These are assumptions of
    our reproducibility requirement.

    \item \emph{Validation before publication:}
    the design requires candidates to satisfy the applicable
    structural and semantic checks before publication
    authorization. SHACL provides the constraint language
    \cite{Knublauch2017SHACL}; the approval prerequisite
    is an institutional rule specified here.
\end{itemize}

Together, these concerns motivate the links among validation,
provenance, and authorization developed in this paper.
They do not prescribe a particular voting protocol
or implementation.

\paragraph{Case Selection and Evidential Role.}

We select the employment-event running case because it brings
several governance challenges into a single, bounded example.
Human participants and software components transform information
from different sources into a versioned RDF graph. Because
automated extraction can produce uncertain or incorrect results,
the graph requires validation before an institution relies on it.
Its subsequent publication, revision, or retirement also requires
decisions about who has authority to change its status. The
analysis therefore concerns how the resulting graph is governed;
it does not assess whether the illustrative WoodHive employment
claim is true.

We derive requirements from the processing stages, assets,
responsibilities, and failure modes described in the running
case, together with the cited literature. To make this derivation
traceable, each requirement attributed to the case must connect
to a responsibility or risk in
Table~\ref{tab:runningcase_actors}, a lifecycle event described
here, or an identified relationship between an asset and its
supporting evidence. These sources provide the basis for the
specialization developed in
Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}--\ref{sec:safeguards}.

The running case helps define the governance problem and
demonstrate the proposed design. Still, the study includes
no interviews, observations of institutional practice, or
results from a deployed DAO. It therefore provides no evidence
of how frequently the identified risks occur, whether
stakeholders accept the design, or how effectively it works
in practice. Applying the design in another domain requires
a separate assessment of that domain's legal, organizational,
and technical constraints. All of this we consider to be future work.

\subsection{Background Literature}
\label{sec:background}

The following concepts define the objects, evidence, and
authority relations used in the design. They complement the
research positioning in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}.

\paragraph{Semantic Assets and Lifecycle Transitions.}

Knowledge graph engineering supports representation, integration,
validation, querying, and publication
\cite{Hogan2021KnowledgeGraphs,Fensel2020KG}.
Here, a \emph{semantic asset} is an identifiable, versioned
RDF dataset, named graph, ontology, set of validation shapes,
mapping, or processing configuration whose institutional
status can change.

To govern a particular graph, the design first needs a way to identify
it. RDF datasets can associate names with graphs
\cite{Cyganiak2014RDFConcepts,Carroll2005NamedGraphs}. The information
within an identified graph is interpreted using concepts and
relationships defined by an ontology or vocabulary, while validation
shapes specify the constraints used to check that information
\cite{Knublauch2017SHACL}. A mapping serves a different purpose in
this paper: it specifies how source records become RDF statements.
Distinguishing these resources matters because each can change
independently and therefore require its own governance decision.

Lifecycle approaches organize recurrent integration, update,
querying, and provenance activities \cite{Geisler2025KGLifecycle}.
For this design, a \emph{lifecycle transition} changes an asset's
institutional status, using the states specified in
Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}. Authorization is a separate
status: a decision can permit a transition without establishing
that it occurs. Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements} and
\ref{sec:dao_mechanisms} apply this distinction to authority
and execution.

\paragraph{Processing History, Validation, and Integrity.}

Here, a \emph{processing trajectory} records the stages, inputs,
configurations, and outputs involved in producing a candidate.
PROV supplies concepts for the entities, activities, and agents
involved in production and derivation
\cite{Moreau2013PROV,Lebo2013PROVO}.
We use those concepts to relate sources, models, processing
activities, and graph outputs. The trajectory is our application
of provenance information, not a separate PROV standard construct.

Validation checks whether the identified candidate satisfies
declared constraints. SHACL provides a language for specifying
such checks \cite{Knublauch2017SHACL}.
Passing validation establishes compliance with those constraints,
rather than factual truth or institutional authority.
Cryptographic hashes provide integrity evidence by identifying
specific graph representations or processing results.

For WoodHive, the processing trajectory records how the article becomes
a candidate graph, and provenance connects the processing activities
to their sources and models. Validation then checks the candidate
against declared constraints, while a content hash supports later
integrity comparison. These mechanisms explain how the graph is
produced and checked. Authorization answers the separate question
of whether the proposed transition is permitted.

\paragraph{Institutional Authority and Decentralized Authorization.}

Role-based access control assigns permissions to organizational
functions \cite{Sandhu1996RBAC}. Collective authorization
additionally requires rules for participation, deliberation,
acceptance, and accountability. DAO mechanisms provide
programmable proposals, voting, quorum, timing, delegation,
and execution
\cite{FaqirRhazoui2020DAOOverview,Hassan2021DAO,Han2025DAOReview}.
Weidener et al. show how governance spanning multiple systems
can obscure decision points \cite{Weidener2026Metagovernance}.
Our design therefore requires an explicit binding between a
decision and its corresponding on-chain or off-chain operation.

In this design, decentralized authorization means a collective
decision under explicit roles and policies, with authority
distributed among independently accountable principals.
It does not require every lifecycle operation to occur
on-chain. For a critical transition, the design requires
at least two independently accountable principals and prevents
one actor from controlling proposal, validation, authorization,
and confirmation end to end. Any exceptional concentration
of authority must be bounded, visible, and reviewable.
The required institutional composition remains specific
to the deployment.

\paragraph{DAO-ML, ET-DM, and DAOMod.}

DAO-ML supplies organizational constructs for roles, committees,
governance areas, permissions, and relations
\cite{Avanzo2024DAOML}. These support specification of who
may participate in a semantic-asset transition.

ET-DM supports both coordination and execution. Its
decision-making and token-economy constructs describe how
collective choices are formed; its smart-contract primitives
and on-chain/off-chain function constructs describe their
execution \cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}. Coordination and Execution
are architectural layers supported by ET-DM, rather than
separate languages.

The 2025 DAOMod article develops ontology, notation, and
methodological guidance for the Organization layer
\cite{Avanzo2025DAOMod}. The integrated Organization,
Coordination, and Execution model is documented in
Avanzo's thesis \cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}.
Related work connects DAO-ML to Solidity
\cite{Avanzo2025DAOMLtoSolidity} and develops token-economy
and voting specifications \cite{Avanzo2026DAOModCoordination}.

\paragraph{Decision and Execution Records.}

Approval can be connected to execution only if the design identifies
what changes, who may act, and what evidence supports the action.
We therefore connect the asset and its lifecycle state to provenance,
integrity, and validation evidence, and relate both to organizational
roles and permissions. The decision and execution mechanisms use
these connections to authorize a particular change and record its result.

Four records document how an approved change proceeds to
execution and what happens when it is attempted:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An \emph{acceptance decision} records the collective
    approval of a proposed change, as specified in a
    transition plan.

    \item An \emph{authorization record} grants permission
    to carry out that approved plan. It records the scope
    of the permission, the supporting evidence, and any
    expiry time.

    \item An \emph{authorization binding} connects the
    permission to the execution service, operation, and
    exact input content. It specifies the content hashes
    and calculation methods used to check that execution
    uses the approved inputs.

    \item An \emph{execution receipt} records the reported
    outcome of an execution attempt, including failure,
    and identifies the executor reporting that outcome.
\end{itemize}

For publication, the accepted decision permits a specific transition
plan. The authorization record preserves that approved scope, while
the binding connects it to the execution service, operation, and exact
input content. The publication adapter can then perform the off-chain
operation and return a receipt for comparison with the authorized plan.
This sequence makes it possible to distinguish permission to publish
from evidence that the intended publication occurs.

These records answer different questions: what is approved,
what is permitted, what is executed, and what result is observed.
An on-chain record alone cannot establish factual correctness,
lawful authority, or faithful off-chain execution.
This distinction guides the safeguards in
Section~\ref{sec:safeguards} and the evaluation in
Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

\subsection{Requirement Derivation and Traceability Protocol}
\label{sec:derivation_protocol}

Following the design-science distinction between the problem environment,
knowledge base, and constructed artifact \cite{hevner2004design}, we
begin requirement derivation by identifying what the running case needs
to govern and where failures can arise. This first stage inventories
assets, lifecycle events, actors, responsibilities, and evidence.
In the second stage, each concern becomes a requirement that identifies
the affected object, eligible or responsible actor, action, conditions,
and evidence needed to check satisfaction.

The third stage connects each requirement to a modeling construct.
DAO-ML provides the organizational roles, scopes, and permissions;
ET-DM provides proposal and collective-decision rules and the execution
of accepted outcomes. Where these constructs are insufficient, we
introduce domain extensions and institutional safeguards. The fourth
stage records whether each construct is inherited, adapted, or newly
introduced, making the contribution distinguishable from its foundations.

The fifth stage checks those choices in both directions. Each case
concern must lead to a requirement and design construct, and each
requirement must have a stated basis in the case, an upstream requirement,
or the cited knowledge base. The separate mapping in
Section~\ref{sec:model_artifact_validation} connects the resulting
requirements to editable models. Its mechanical checks make the links
inspectable but do not independently validate the derivation.

We apply the procedure in sequence across
Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}--\ref{sec:safeguards}.
The Organization-layer requirements (OLR1--OLR12) provide
inputs to the Coordination and Execution requirements
(CR1--CR6 and ER1--ER6). These requirements expose the risks
addressed by the safeguards (SG1--SG12).

At each stage, we merge candidates that concern the same
object, specify the same control, and require the same evidence.
We exclude a candidate if it only restates an implementation
choice, lacks a basis in the case or knowledge base, or belongs
to a later layer. The consolidated tables record each
requirement's identifier, wording, model treatment, and rationale,
allowing another analyst to inspect and repeat the derivation.

\paragraph{Coverage and Assessment Limits.}

Completeness is assessed against the stated case and model boundary.
The derivation must account for every actor, responsibility, and failure
mode in Table~\ref{tab:runningcase_actors}; otherwise, a relevant concern
could disappear between the case description and the design.
For the same reason, each lifecycle event in
Section~\ref{sec:runningcase} must have an identifiable authority,
decision process, execution outcome, and evidence trail.

Successful, rejected, failed, stale, challenged, and exceptional
paths must lead to defined statuses that specify their outcomes
and permitted next actions. Each requirement must also be
traceable to its source and the constructs that address it.

Table~\ref{tab:derivation_coverage} records the questions used
in the authors' analytical walkthrough and the requirement
families addressing them. The walkthrough identifies coverage
for the stated case elements. It does not involve an independent
requirements panel or a measurement of agreement between
assessors. The assessment therefore supports coverage within
the stated analytical boundary, rather than stakeholder
acceptance, legal sufficiency, or completeness for other domains.
It also does not establish that further empirical investigation
reveals no additional requirements.

A DAO-ML governance area specifies the scope within which
roles and committees exercise authority
\cite{Avanzo2024DAOML}. In our specialization, that scope
covers identifiable semantic assets, using the concepts
introduced in Section~\ref{sec:background}. These assets
can include a graph partition describing employment events,
a mapping to EMTAK economic-activity categories, a set of
graph-validation constraints, or the configuration used
to extract information from source articles. Associating
an asset with a governance area makes explicit which
organizational authority applies to it.

Our institutional specialization introduces
\emph{SemanticAsset} to represent an identifiable resource
subject to governance. The design assigns each asset a stable
identifier and a type. Each immutable version has its own
identifier and a reference to its off-chain representation,
allowing permissions and decisions to refer to a specific
version.
Provenance records and validation reports normally provide
supporting evidence for a graph version. When these records
require their own approval, revision, or retirement procedures,
the design also treats them as separately governed semantic
assets.

Let $A$ be the set of assets, $V_a$ the versions of asset
$a\in A$, and $V=\bigcup_{a\in A}V_a$.
The function $owner:V\rightarrow A$ identifies the asset to
which each version belongs. Let $O$ denote the permitted
lifecycle operations. Equation~\ref{eq:lifecycle_states}
defines the asset-state vocabulary:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:lifecycle_states}
\begin{aligned}
S=\{&\mathit{candidate},\mathit{validated},\mathit{published},\\
&\mathit{superseded},\mathit{deprecated},\mathit{archived}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In the notation below, $\in$ means ``belongs to,'' $\cup$
combines sets, $\times$ forms combinations of their elements,
and $\rightarrow$ describes a mapping from inputs to outputs.
Braces list a set; angle brackets $\langle\cdots\rangle$ group fields into a record.
These conventions distinguish state vocabularies and structured
records from conditions that must be checked.

An atomic version transition is
$\tau=(v,s_i,o,s_j)\in V\times S\times O\times S$.
Its four components identify the version, source state,
operation, and target state.

A transition plan is a non-empty finite set
$g=\{\tau_1,\ldots,\tau_n\}$.
The predicate $PlanFor(g,a)$ holds when all affected versions
belong to asset $a$, no version has conflicting source or
target states, and the operations satisfy the lifecycle
invariants. These invariants include retaining at most one
current authoritative version after confirmation.
All transitions in the plan must be confirmed together
or remain unconfirmed.

A transition plan specifies the lifecycle changes to be
authorized and carried out. An \emph{elementary plan} contains
one transition, such as publishing a validated graph version.
A \emph{compound plan} contains several related transitions.
For example, replacing a published graph requires publishing
the new version and marking the previous version as
superseded. Restoring a retained version similarly requires
returning it to authoritative use and withdrawing the current
version from that role. These changes belong to one plan
because they must be coordinated.
We use $T$ to denote the set of named plan types and
$type(g)$ to identify the type assigned to a particular
plan $g$.

For example, let $v_{old}$ be the published WoodHive graph
and $v_{new}$ a validated correction. Replacement combines
$(v_{new},\mathit{validated},\mathit{publish},\mathit{published})$
with
$(v_{old},\mathit{published},\mathit{supersede},\mathit{superseded})$.
Confirming only one change could leave two authoritative
versions or none.

In this design, correction creates a new candidate rather than
altering a published version in place. Dibowski demonstrates
change-history tracking and recovery of past versions
\cite{Dibowski2024TraceabilityKG}; PROV represents generation
and derivation relations \cite{Moreau2013PROV,Lebo2013PROVO}.
We add the requirement that restoration use a new authorized
transition while preserving the intervening governance history.
Authorization remains separate from the asset states in
Equation~\ref{eq:lifecycle_states}.

\subsection{Specialized Roles, Committees, and Organizational Relations}
\label{sec:roles}

We retain DAO-ML roles, committees, and organizational relations
\cite{Avanzo2024DAOML}, while assigning them the lifecycle
responsibilities identified in Table~\ref{tab:runningcase_actors}. Roles describe functions rather than
local job titles. One person can hold several roles, and actors
from different institutions can hold the same role, subject
to explicit incompatibility and distribution constraints.

The seven responsibilities follow the work needed to produce, approve,
and maintain a governed graph. A \emph{Producer} supplies source content
or initiates a candidate, and a \emph{Curator} prepares, corrects, and
versions the resulting semantic content. Because changes to generation
can alter that content, a \emph{ModelMaintainer} is responsible for the
models, prompts, thresholds, and vocabularies used in production.

Checking the result and permitting its use remain separate responsibilities.
A \emph{Validator} produces validation evidence, while an
\emph{Authorizer} participates in the decision permitting a lifecycle
transition. An \emph{Auditor} can inspect the records and initiate a
challenge when the evidence or procedure is disputed. Where an emergency
procedure is needed, an optional \emph{EmergencyAuthority} exercises
only the exceptional powers expressly assigned to it.

These responsibilities do not imply additional permissions.
Validation does not authorize publication, and an auditor's
ability to inspect or challenge does not automatically grant
veto or revocation rights. Execution is assigned through
a separate permission to an eligible actor or service.

A DAO-ML committee represents an identifiable collective body.
For example, a PublicationCommittee can include authorizers
from participating institutions, while a ValidationCommittee
can combine technical and domain expertise. Membership and
governance-area relations specify organizational responsibility.
Quorum, voting weights, decision rules, and timing belong
to the ET-DM specialization. Table~\ref{tab:governance_roles} maps the specialized
responsibilities to their DAO-ML foundations.

\subsection{Transition-Scoped Permissions and Decision Rights}
\label{sec:decision_rights}

Permissions must identify both the affected asset and the
type of lifecycle change. Equation~\ref{eq:permission_vocabulary}
defines the action vocabulary:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:permission_vocabulary}
\begin{aligned}
P=\{&\mathit{propose},\mathit{prepare},\mathit{validate},
\mathit{authorize},\\
&\mathit{execute},\mathit{challenge},\mathit{delegate},
\mathit{administer}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Validation, authorization, and execution have separate permissions
because they answer different questions. Validation records whether
specified checks are performed; authorization permits a transition
under the decision policy; and execution performs or requests the
operation. An actor who can perform one of these functions therefore
receives no automatic permission to perform the others.

Permissions depend on who acts, which asset is affected,
and what change is proposed. For example, a committee may
have permission to authorize publication of a particular
graph without having permission to execute that publication
or approve changes to other graphs.

Let $R$ denote the set of roles, $C$ the set of committees,
$A$ the set of governed assets, $T$ the set of transition-plan
types, and $P$ the set of possible permissions.
Equation~\ref{eq:decision_rights} defines a function $\delta$
that assigns a set of permissions to each combination of
role or committee, asset, and plan type:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decision_rights}
\delta:(R\cup C)\times A\times T\rightarrow 2^P.
\end{equation}
Here, $2^P$ denotes the set of all subsets of $P$.
Thus, $\delta(x,a,t)$ returns the permissions assigned to
role or committee $x$ for asset $a$ and plan type $t$.
The returned set can contain several permissions or be empty.

Equation~\ref{eq:organizational_eligibility} then checks
whether a particular permission $\ell$ belongs to that set:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:organizational_eligibility}
Eligible(x,a,t,\ell)\iff \ell\in\delta(x,a,t),
\end{equation}
where $x\in R\cup C$, $a\in A$, $t\in T$, and $\ell\in P$.
The symbol $\iff$ means ``if and only if'': eligibility holds
exactly when the permission belongs to the assigned set.
For example, a publication committee is eligible to authorize
publication of the WoodHive graph if its assigned permission
set for that graph and plan type contains the authorization
permission.

This eligibility concerns the permissions assigned to a role
or committee. An individual actor exercises those permissions
through valid membership. Eligibility alone does not mean
that a particular proposal is approved or that its execution
is authorized; those decisions require the procedures
specified in the Coordination and Execution layers.

For example, a curator can be eligible to propose reprocessing
of the WoodHive graph without being eligible to authorize
publication of the result. A committee can authorize
deprecation without being assigned the operation that
implements withdrawal from active use.

Eligibility alone does not establish acceptance.
Evidence, quorum, timing, and execution conditions are
specified in Section~\ref{sec:dao_mechanisms} and qualified
by Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}.

Table~\ref{tab:decision_rights} illustrates the permission
separation in Equation~\ref{eq:decision_rights}. The assignments
are examples rather than a universal staffing prescription.

The organizational model records these limits; the coordination

\subsection{Consolidated Organization-Layer Requirements}
\label{sec:constraints}

Equation~\ref{eq:organization_specialization} summarizes
the organizational specification for knowledge graph
governance, denoted by $\mathcal{O}_{KG}$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:organization_specialization}
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal{O}_{KG}=\langle&D,U,\Pi,R,C,G,A,S,T,P,E,\\
&\rho,\sigma,\delta,\epsilon,\chi\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $D$ is the DAO, $U$ the participating actors, and
$\Pi$ the accountable principals, such as institutions or
independently mandated persons. The remaining sets identify
roles $R$, committees $C$, governance areas $G$, assets $A$,
states $S$, transition types $T$, permissions $P$, and
evidence categories $E$. The tuple describes the organizational
model rather than an execution procedure.

The relations specify how these elements connect:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\rho:U\rightarrow 2^{R\cup C}$ assigns actors
    to roles and committees. An associated identity function,
    $principal:U\rightarrow\Pi$, connects actors to accountable
    principals.

    \item $\sigma:G\rightarrow 2^{A\times T}$ associates
    governance areas with assets and transition types.

    \item $\delta$ assigns the permissions defined in
    Equation~\ref{eq:decision_rights}.

    \item $\epsilon:T\rightarrow 2^E$ identifies the evidence
    categories required for each transition type.

    \item $\chi:T\rightarrow\mathcal{C}_{dist}$ identifies
    the applicable distribution constraint, where
    $\mathcal{C}_{dist}$ is the set of permitted constraint
    specifications.
\end{itemize}

For the WoodHive graph, these relations identify the responsible
curator and publication committee, their scoped permissions,
and the required provenance, content hash, model version,
and validation evidence.

Each transition type also references its coordination policy.
The reference identifies the applicable decision procedure
and whether review, quorum, a voting period, execution delay,
or escalation is required. Protocol details and numerical
settings are specified in Section~\ref{sec:dao_mechanisms}.

Some lifecycle transitions change which information an
institution treats as authoritative. We classify these as
critical when their consequences require shared authority
under the applicable governance policy. For example,
replacing the published WoodHive graph with a corrected
version changes the employment information available for
institutional analysis.

For such transitions, $\chi(t)$ requires participation by
at least two independent organizational principals and
prevents any single actor from controlling the complete
approval-and-execution process:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:distributed_transition}
\begin{aligned}
&Distributed(t)\\
&\quad\iff |IndependentPrincipals(t)|\geq 2\\
&\qquad\land\neg\exists u\in U\;EndToEndControl(u,t).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $t\in T$ is a transition-plan type, not an individual
proposal. $Distributed(t)$ checks the organizational allocation
for that type; the actual participants in a particular decision
are checked separately in Equation~\ref{eq:decentralized_decision}.
The symbol $\land$ means ``and,'' so both conditions must hold.
The bars $|\cdot|$ count distinct principals, $\geq$ means
``at least,'' and $\neg\exists$ means ``there is no.''
$IndependentPrincipals(t)\subseteq\Pi$ identifies principals
with valid mandates and relevant role or committee assignments.
$EndToEndControl(u,t)$ means that actor $u$ controls proposal,
validation, authorization, execution, and confirmation.
The constraint therefore requires at least two independently
accountable principals and excludes sole control of the
complete process. Multiple addresses controlled by one principal
do not establish this distribution.
For example, assigning WoodHive publication authority to independently
mandated institutions B and C can provide the required organizational
structure, provided no actor controls the complete process.
Whether their representatives actually participate in a specific
publication decision requires a separate check.

A deployment can require a larger body or participation from
several institutions. Any exceptional relaxation must be
explicitly bounded and reviewable under
Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}. The organizational specification defines the required
distribution of authority. The coordination, execution,
and safeguard mechanisms must enforce this requirement.

\section{ET-DM Coordination and Execution Specialization for Knowledge Graph Lifecycles}

Addressing RQ2 therefore requires explicit connections between

This section follows the process from proposing a lifecycle
change to checking its execution.
Section~\ref{sec:etdm_inputs} first explains how the

Section~\ref{sec:dao_decisions} then specifies how changes
are proposed, who may participate in decisions, and how
proposals are accepted or rejected.
Section~\ref{sec:smart_contracts} explains how an accepted
proposal leads to an authorization and which conditions
must hold before execution can begin.
Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_binding} connects that authorization
to the specific operation, execution service, and input
content, and explains how an execution receipt supports
verification of the result.
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:onchain_offchain} explains how
blockchain components and external services work together
and brings together the Coordination requirements

\subsection{Specialization Approach and Organizational Inputs}
\label{sec:etdm_inputs}

Coordination begins with the authority specified by OLR1--OLR12 in
Section~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}. The asset versions and
transition types establish what a proposal may concern, while roles,
committees, and scoped permissions determine who may participate.
Evidence categories, policy references, and distribution constraints
then supply conditions for considering and accepting that proposal.
Execution uses the resulting acceptance to determine which operation
is permitted and what must be checked after it is attempted.

We inherit ET-DM decision-protocol, token-economy, smart-contract,
and on-chain/off-chain function constructs as modeling foundations.
We adapt them to semantic assets, lifecycle transitions, and
institutional responsibilities. Additional authorization,
binding, and receipt constructs specify the connections needed
to establish what a decision permits and what execution produces.
As in Section~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}, these are
platform-independent design requirements; their implementation
support is assessed in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}.

graph infrastructure. The dashed return arrow closes the loop:
it carries the receipt, result hash, and observed state needed to check
what the operation produces. The Coordination and Execution boxes
thus represent two architectural layers supported by ET-DM.

\subsection{ET-DM Coordination-Layer Specialization}
\label{sec:dao_decisions}

Coordination defines how organizationally eligible participants
reach a decision on a transition proposal. The proposal includes
an \emph{evidence manifest}: a list of supporting records and
their stable references, such as provenance, validation results,
model versions, and content hashes.

The WoodHive examples illustrate how this design would work; they are
not additional executed tests. In the supplied instance, principal A
produces and executes publication, while the committee comprises
synthetic principals B and C. Their example policy requires both
principals and unanimous approval, with the authority monitor limiting
one principal's share to 0.5. These choices illustrate distributed
responsibility without claiming that independent institutions actually
participate in the evaluation.

Equation~\ref{eq:transition_proposal} defines the proposal:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:transition_proposal}
p=\langle id,a,g,e,i,\kappa\rangle.
\end{equation}

Here, $id$ identifies the proposal, $a$ identifies the asset,
$g$ is the complete transition plan from
Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}, $e$ is the evidence manifest,
$i\in U$ is the initiator, and $\kappa$ references the
coordination policy. The initiator's assignments are given
by $\rho(i)$. Admission requires $PlanFor(g,a)$.

The plan identifies every affected version, expected source
and target state, and operation. For publication of the
WoodHive graph, the proposal fixes the validated version
and its supporting evidence. Replacement or restoration
also fixes the coupled changes to the other affected version.
Changing the plan requires a new proposal or an explicitly
recorded amendment procedure.

For example, a curator proposes publishing WoodHive graph version
$v_1$, which describes the illustrative creation of 120 jobs.
The proposal identifies $v_1$, the change from validated to published,
the curator, the applicable policy, and the supporting validation
and provenance records. Approval of this proposal does not also
approve a later corrected version $v_2$.

Equation~\ref{eq:coordination_policy} specifies the policy
for transition type $t$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coordination_policy}
\begin{aligned}
\kappa_t=\langle&
Elig_t,M_t,Q_t,\Theta_t,W_t,Del_t,\\
&I_t,N_t,C_t\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $\kappa_t$ groups the decision rules for plan type $t$.
The subscript $t$ means that different plan types can use different
rules. The components define:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $Elig_t$: eligible roles and committees;
    \item $M_t$: the decision mechanism;
    \item $Q_t$: the quorum rule;
    \item $\Theta_t$: the acceptance rule;
    \item $W_t$: review and decision windows;
    \item $Del_t$: delegation conditions;
    \item $I_t$: the basis for assessing independence;
    \item $N_t$: the minimum independent-principal count; and
    \item $C_t$: the maximum effective authority share
    of one principal.
\end{itemize}

For critical transitions, $N_t>1$ and $C_t<1$.
The independence assessment uses the accountable principals
and mandates defined in Section~\ref{sec:constraints};
distinct roles or addresses alone do not establish independence.
ET-DM supplies the protocol and token-economy foundations,
while the specialization connects their parameters to
transition types and organizational mandates
\cite{Avanzo2025ETDM,Avanzo2026DAOModCoordination}.

The decision policy must distribute actual decision-making
authority, rather than merely require several participants.
Two participants may represent the same institution, or one
participant may control most of the decision weight.
Equation~\ref{eq:decentralized_decision} therefore checks
independence, concentration of authority, and control over
the complete process.

For a proposal $p$, let $a(p)$ denote the affected asset
and $g(p)$ its proposed transition plan. The expression
$t(p)=type(g(p))$ identifies the plan type, such as publication,
replacement, or restoration. This type selects the applicable
decision policy, $\kappa_{t(p)}$.

The equation uses the following quantities:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $P_p=CountedParticipants(p)$ is the set of admitted
    actors whose participation counts toward the decision.

    \item $I_p=I_{t(p)}$ is the policy's rule for determining
    which organizational principals count as independent.
    Actors representing the same principal do not become
    independent merely by using different addresses.

    \item $IndependentCount(P_p,I_p)$ is the number of
    independent principals represented by the counted
    participants, assessed using rule $I_p$.

    \item $N_{t(p)}$ is the minimum number of independent
    principals required by the policy.

    \item $MaxAuthorityShare(P_p,I_p)$ is the largest fraction
    of the counted decision weight controlled by one principal.
    Role assignments and valid delegations determine which
    contributions belong to each principal.

    \item $C_{t(p)}$ is the largest authority share that the
    policy permits one principal to control.

    \item $EndToEndControl(u,t(p))$ means that actor $u$
    controls proposal, validation, authorization, execution,
    and confirmation for this plan type. Here, $U$ is the
    set of actors.
\end{itemize}

A decision satisfies the distribution requirement only
when all three conditions below hold:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decentralized_decision}
\begin{aligned}
&DecentralizedDecision(p,\kappa_{t(p)})\\
&\quad\iff IndependentCount(P_p,I_p)\geq N_{t(p)}\\
&\qquad\land MaxAuthorityShare(P_p,I_p)\leq C_{t(p)}\\
&\qquad\land\neg\exists u\in U\;
EndToEndControl(u,t(p)).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The three conditions work together: enough independent principals
must participate, none may hold more than the permitted decision weight,
and no actor may control the entire process. Counting participants alone
would miss both concentrated voting weight and control over the other
stages of governance.

For example, suppose the WoodHive publication policy requires
at least two independent principals and permits each to
control at most half of the counted decision weight.
Representatives of institutions B and C participate with
equal weight. The first condition holds because
$IndependentCount(P_p,I_p)=2$ meets $N_{t(p)}=2$.
The second holds because
$MaxAuthorityShare(P_p,I_p)=0.5$ does not exceed
$C_{t(p)}=0.5$. The third still requires checking that
neither actor controls the complete process.
Two addresses representing institution B would fail the
first condition, regardless of how many votes they submit.

%For illustration, suppose the WoodHive publication policy requires two independent principals with equal decision weight and permits no principal to hold more than half of the counted weight. Participation by representatives of principals A and B can satisfy these numerical conditions. Two addresses controlled by A cannot: they still represent one principal. The separate prohibition on one actor controlling the entire process must also hold.

The structural condition $Distributed(t)$ in
Equation~\ref{eq:distributed_transition} establishes that the
organizational allocation supports distributed authority.
Equation~\ref{eq:decentralized_decision} checks whether a
particular decision meets that condition in practice.
Acceptance of a critical transition requires both.
Neither condition establishes legal legitimacy or excludes
informal influence.

Eligibility normally derives from institutional roles and
committee assignments. A token-weighted ballot is optional.
Token-economy constructs can represent credentials, reputation,
or other justified decision weights, but a transferable
financial token is not assumed to confer institutional
authority over semantic assets.

A proposal's progress and its final decision are recorded
separately. The \emph{process status} indicates where the
proposal is in the decision procedure. A proposal can be
a draft, submitted for consideration, under review,
temporarily suspended, or closed.

The \emph{terminal outcome} records how the procedure ends.
A proposal can be accepted, rejected, expired because its
decision period ends without resolution, or canceled
under the applicable policy. A closed process therefore
does not necessarily mean an accepted proposal.

Equation~\ref{eq:decision_process_status} lists the possible
values. Here, $ProcessStatus(p)$ denotes the current process
status of proposal $p$, while $Decide(p,\kappa_{t(p)})$
denotes its terminal outcome under the policy applicable
to its plan type. %The symbol $\in$ means ``is one of.''
The terminal outcome is assigned when the procedure ends.
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:decision_process_status}
\begin{aligned}
ProcessStatus(p)\in\{&
\mathit{draft},\mathit{submitted},\mathit{review},\\
&\mathit{suspended},\mathit{closed}\},\\
Decide(p,\kappa_{t(p)})\in\{&
\mathit{accepted},\mathit{rejected},\\
&\mathit{expired},\mathit{cancelled}\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

For example, a proposal to publish the WoodHive graph
moves from draft to submitted and then to review.
A challenge to the reported job count can suspend review.
After the challenge is resolved, review can resume.
If the committee approves the proposal, its process status
becomes closed and its outcome is accepted. This records
approval; it does not mean that publication has occurred.

The worked JSON profile uses $\mathit{admitted}$ for an admitted
submission, $\mathit{challenged}$ for a recorded challenge, and
$\mathit{completed}$ for closure. These labels summarize parts
of the fuller process vocabulary above: completed does not itself
mean accepted. The profile illustrates accepted, rejected, and
expired outcomes; cancellation remains a design alternative rather
than an additional worked trace.

The move from submission to review is conditional: the proposer must
hold the scoped permission required by OLR6--OLR7 and provide the
evidence categories required by OLR9. A recorded challenge can then
suspend consideration, so resolution must either return the proposal
to review or close it with a terminal outcome. Acceptance additionally
requires the applicable eligibility, evidence, quorum, threshold,
timing, delegation, distribution, and challenge-resolution conditions
to be satisfied; it does not establish execution of the operation.


Before a participant's vote or approval can count toward a
decision, the system must check the participant's authority.
The participant must hold a role or committee assignment with
the relevant authorization permission and a valid institutional
mandate. If the participant acts through delegated authority,
that delegation must also be valid.

Equation~\ref{eq:counted_participation} expresses these necessary
conditions for actor $u$ and proposal $p$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:counted_participation}
\begin{aligned}
Counted(u,p)\Rightarrow{}&
\exists x\in\rho(u):\\
&Eligible\bigl(x,a(p),t(p),\mathit{authorize}\bigr)\\
&\land MandateValid(u,p)\\
&\land\bigl(\neg Delegated(u,p)\\
&\qquad\lor DelegationValid(u,p,\kappa_{t(p)})\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $Counted(u,p)$ means that actor $u$'s participation
contributes to the decision on proposal $p$. The implication
$\Rightarrow$ states that whenever participation counts, the
conditions on the right must hold. These conditions alone do
not guarantee that participation counts: the decision policy
may impose additional requirements.

The first condition requires at least one qualifying
organizational assignment. The set $\rho(u)$ contains the roles
and committee memberships assigned to actor $u$.
The expression $\exists x\in\rho(u)$ means that at least one
of these assignments must provide authorization permission
for the proposal's asset $a(p)$ and transition-plan type $t(p)$.

The second condition, $MandateValid(u,p)$, requires the actor's
institutional mandate to cover the proposed action and remain
valid at the time of participation. The final condition requires
either that the actor is not acting through delegation or that
the delegation satisfies the applicable policy $\kappa_{t(p)}$.
The symbols $\lor$ and $\neg$ mean ``or'' and ``not,''
respectively. Delegation checks cover the scope,
validity interval, and conditions recorded in $Del_{t(p)}$.

For example, a representative's approval of a proposal to publish
the WoodHive graph counts only if the representative holds an
assignment permitting authorization of that publication and has
a valid institutional mandate. If the representative acts on
behalf of another participant, the delegation must also be valid
for this proposal. An expired mandate or an out-of-scope delegation
prevents the approval from counting, even when the representative
can submit a technically valid signature.

Incompatible assignments are checked before participation counts.
The additional institutional controls for conflicts, capture, and
emergency authority are specified in Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}.

\subsection{ET-DM Execution-Layer Specialization}
\label{sec:smart_contracts}

Execution translates an accepted decision into a scoped
authorization and an operation binding. ET-DM smart-contract
primitives and on-chain/off-chain function concepts provide
the foundations
\cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}.

An accepted proposal records agreement to a change, but execution
also requires a precise record of what that agreement permits.
The governance component therefore issues an \emph{authorization
record}. This record connects the approval decision to the approved
transition plan, fixes the supporting evidence, and specifies when
the permission expires. Its scope must be enforced so that an
approval cannot be reused for a different change or altered evidence.

Equation~\ref{eq:authorization_record} defines the information
contained in this record:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:authorization_record}
\begin{aligned}
z=\langle&
decisionId,policyId,assetId,g,\\
&eHash,eHashMethod,expiresAt\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Here, $z$ denotes the authorization record. Its fields mean:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $decisionId$ identifies the accepted decision from which
    the permission derives.
    \item $policyId$ identifies the governance policy under which
    the proposal is accepted.
    \item $assetId$ identifies the governed semantic asset.
    \item $g$ is the approved transition plan, specifying the affected
    versions, expected source and target states, and operations.
    \item $eHash$ is a cryptographic digest of the evidence manifest,
    which lists supporting records, their references, and content hashes.
    Comparing digests allows changes to that manifest to be detected.
    \item $eHashMethod$ identifies the hashing method used to calculate
    and verify $eHash$. The manifest also requires a deterministic
    encoding so that this comparison is reproducible.
    \item $expiresAt$ specifies the expiry time. After expiry, the
    authorization cannot admit a new execution attempt.
\end{itemize}

For example, suppose a committee accepts a proposal to publish
WoodHive graph version $v_1$. The authorization identifies that
decision, the applicable policy, the WoodHive asset, and the plan
to move $v_1$ from validated to published. It also records the
digest of the manifest referencing the supporting provenance and
validation evidence, together with the hashing method and expiry.
It does not permit publication of a different version $v_2$.
If the manifest changes, the digest comparison fails and the
existing authorization cannot be used without reassessment.
Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_binding} connects this permission to
the designated execution service and exact input content.

The formal record specifies required information rather than the
literal field layout of the compact JSON example. In that example,
\texttt{authorization.decision}, \texttt{plan}, and \texttt{expires}
reference the decision, approved plan, and expiry; the executor and
nonce are also explicit. The asset, policy, and evidence are reached
through linked records. The example does not provide a separately
computed evidence-manifest digest for every field in $z$.
Its repeated content digest is a marked synthetic placeholder,
not a hash measured from the WoodHive graph. The ontology declares
concepts and selected relations; it does not encode every field
or enforce every condition in these equations.

The authorization must remain connected to operations that can occur
outside the blockchain. On-chain functions maintain governance and
status records, while off-chain functions perform work such as RDF
validation, publication, catalogue updates, provenance preservation,
and archival. A hybrid binding connects them by specifying which
executor may act, what inputs and preconditions it must use, and what
result and receipt it must return. Timeout behavior is specified as well,
so failure to complete the operation remains visible.

An authorization record alone does not establish that execution may
begin. The system must check that the approval remains valid and
that the graph and supporting evidence still match what is authorized.
Equation~\ref{eq:execution_enabled} defines when authorization $z$
passes these admission checks for a new execution attempt:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:execution_enabled}
\begin{aligned}
&ExecEnabled(z)\\
&\quad\iff Accepted(z.decisionId)\\
&\qquad\land Unexpired(z)\\
&\qquad\land SourceStatesMatch(z.g)\\
&\qquad\land H_{z.eHashMethod}\!\bigl(\\[-1mm]
&\qquad\quad CurrentEvidenceManifest(z)\bigr)\\[-1mm]
&\hspace{33mm}{}=z.eHash\\
&\qquad\land\neg Consumed(z).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

The expression $ExecEnabled(z)$ means that authorization $z$ permits
admission of a new execution attempt under these checks. All
five conditions must hold. The notation $z.field$ selects a field
of the authorization record. The conditions mean:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $Accepted(z.decisionId)$ checks that the referenced decision
    has an accepted outcome. Submission alone does not satisfy it;
    neither does a rejected, expired, or cancelled outcome.
    \item $Unexpired(z)$ checks that the authorization remains within
    the validity period specified by $z.expiresAt$.
    \item $SourceStatesMatch(z.g)$ checks every transition in the
    approved plan $z.g$. Each affected version must still have the
    source state expected by the plan. A different current state
    makes the plan's starting assumptions outdated.
    \item The hash equality checks that the current evidence manifest
    matches the manifest fixed by the authorization.
    $CurrentEvidenceManifest(z)$ returns that manifest in its specified
    deterministic encoding. $H$ denotes the hash function; the subscript
    $z.eHashMethod$ selects the recorded hashing method. The calculated
    digest must equal $z.eHash$, where $=$ means equality. Referenced
    evidence must additionally be checked against its committed content
    hashes: an unchanged reference alone does not establish unchanged
    evidence content.
    \item $\neg Consumed(z)$ checks that the authorization has not
    already been used or reserved for an execution attempt. Resuming an already admitted attempt follows
    the binding and retry rules in Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_binding};
    it does not admit a second attempt under the same authorization.
\end{itemize}

For example, suppose $z$ permits publication of WoodHive version $v_1$
from the validated state. Before publication begins, the system checks
that the committee decision is accepted, the authorization has not
expired, and $v_1$ is still validated. It recalculates the manifest
digest and compares it with the approved digest, and checks that
$z$ has not already been consumed. If another operation changes
$v_1$'s lifecycle state, the source-state check fails. If the manifest
changes, the digest comparison fails. If an earlier attempt has
reserved $z$, another new attempt cannot use it. Failure of any
condition prevents admission under this authorization.

Passing these checks permits an attempt; it does not establish
successful publication, factual correctness, or legal compliance.
The binding specifies the execution service and input content,
and the receipt and confirmation checks assess the observed result.
Section~\ref{sec:safeguards} specifies additional institutional
conditions.

Authorization and execution status remain separate.
An admitted operation can remain pending, time out,
encounter a stale source, produce mismatched content,
or fail in an external service. These outcomes must be
recorded so that acceptance cannot be mistaken for completion.

\subsection{Binding Decisions to Technically Confirmed Lifecycle Transitions}
\label{sec:lifecycle_binding}

The binding identifies the execution service, operation,
and exact content covered by the authorization:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:execution_binding}
\begin{aligned}
b=\langle&
bindingId,z,adapterId,operation,\\
&inputManifest,canonMethod,hashMethod\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:execution_binding} defines the execution binding.
Here, $b$ is the binding; $bindingId$ is its unique identifier;
$z$ is the authorization it implements; $adapterId$ identifies
the execution service; and $operation$ identifies the requested
action. The $inputManifest$ lists the required input versions and
their expected content hashes. The fields $canonMethod$ and
$hashMethod$ identify the canonicalization and hashing methods.
Together, these fields make the execution request specific enough
to compare with the permission granted by $z$.

The canonicalization method produces a deterministic
representation for RDF integrity comparison:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:expected_content_hash}
\begin{aligned}
ExpectedHash_b(v)={}&H_{b.hashMethod}\!\bigl(\\[-1mm]
&Canon_{b.canonMethod}(Content(v))\bigr).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In Equation~\ref{eq:expected_content_hash}, the symbol $v$ identifies a version, and $Content(v)$ returns its
RDF content. The function $Canon$ converts that content into a
deterministic representation using the method $b.canonMethod$.
The function $H$ then hashes that representation using
$b.hashMethod$. The result, $ExpectedHash_b(v)$, is the expected
digest for version $v$ under binding $b$. The notation $b.field$
selects a field of the binding. The equality defines the digest;
it does not itself compare a published result with that digest.

A binding is well formed when it passes three checks.
First, its approved transition plan must apply to the
identified asset. This is expressed by
$PlanFor(b.z.g,b.z.assetId)$, where $b.z$ is the authorization
referenced by binding $b$, $b.z.g$ is the approved plan,
and $b.z.assetId$ identifies the governed asset.

Second, the input manifest must list all versions whose
content is needed to carry out the plan, without omissions
or unrelated additions. Third, the expected hash recorded
for each listed version $v$ must equal $ExpectedHash_b(v)$,
calculated using the canonicalization and hashing methods
specified in the binding.

Recording both methods makes the content comparison
reproducible. The same RDF graph can be written with its
statements in a different order or with different local
labels for blank nodes. Blank nodes represent resources
without globally assigned identifiers; changing their local
labels need not change the graph they describe. Nevertheless,
hashing these different text representations directly can
produce different digests. Canonicalization converts them
into a consistent representation before hashing.

For example, a binding to publish WoodHive version $v_1$
must reference an approved plan for the WoodHive asset and
list $v_1$ with its expected content hash. Reordering the
RDF statements leaves the hash unchanged after
canonicalization. Changing the reported job count from
120 to 150 changes the graph content, so comparison with
the approved hash should detect the alteration.

Once the admission checks pass, the design must prevent another request
from using the same permission. Execution therefore creates a durable
admission record and atomically reserves the authorization for binding
$b$. We write $Admitted(b,q)$ when admission record $q$ exists.
A repeated request with the same $bindingId$ resumes or returns that
attempt; a different binding cannot reuse its reservation. Because
reservation changes the authorization's state, confirmation checks the
admission record rather than requiring the authorization to remain
unconsumed after execution.

After attempting the operation, the adapter returns
a signed or otherwise attributable receipt:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:execution_receipt}
\begin{aligned}
r=\langle&
bindingId,admissionId,status,\\
&resultManifest,time,\\
&executorId,errorCode\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:execution_receipt} specifies the fields of receipt
$r$. The $bindingId$ links it to the execution request, and
$admissionId$ links it to the recorded admission of that attempt.
The $status$ records the reported outcome. The $resultManifest$
lists observed result states and applicable content hashes;
$time$ records when the result is reported; $executorId$ identifies
the reporting executor; and $errorCode$ identifies an error where
applicable.
A receipt is a report that must be checked, rather than proof of
success merely because it exists.

The worked JSON receipt uses \texttt{attempt} for the attempt
reference, \texttt{executor} for the reporting principal,
\texttt{result\_manifest} for the observed results, and
\texttt{failure} for failure information. Its status
\texttt{success} corresponds to $\mathit{succeeded}$ here.
The compact example does not separately instantiate every formal
receipt field, including $time$. These naming correspondences
support interpretation, not a claim of complete schema equivalence.

The receipt status includes at least \emph{succeeded},
\emph{failed}, \emph{timed-out}, \emph{hash-mismatch},
and \emph{stale-source}. For every authorized transition,
the result manifest records the observed post-state and,
where applicable, the canonical output hash.

After publishing $v_1$, the WoodHive adapter reports the attempt's
identifier, the observed publication state, the content hash,
and its own identity. If the stored content differs from the
approved content, the receipt reports a hash mismatch rather
than a successful publication.

$ResultMatches(b,r)$ holds when the receipt covers exactly
the authorized plan, all observed states match the target
states, and all applicable output hashes match the committed
content hashes. The receipt must be attributable to the
designated executor and linked to the decision, authorization,
asset versions, and provenance.

Technical confirmation requires:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:technical_confirmation}
\begin{aligned}
&TechnicalConfirmed(b,r)\\
&\quad\iff Admitted(b,r.admissionId)\\
&\qquad\land r.bindingId=b.bindingId\\
&\qquad\land r.status=\mathit{succeeded}\\
&\qquad\land ResultMatches(b,r).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:technical_confirmation} requires four checks for
binding $b$ and receipt $r$. Every listed check must pass.
$Admitted(b,r.admissionId)$ requires an admission record for this
binding and the attempt identified by the receipt. The equality
$r.bindingId=b.bindingId$ requires the receipt and binding to
identify the same request. The equality
$r.status=\mathit{succeeded}$ requires a successful reported
outcome. Finally, $ResultMatches(b,r)$ requires the observed states
and applicable hashes to match the complete authorized plan.
The notation $r.field$ or $b.field$ selects a record field.
Thus, a successful status alone cannot establish confirmation.

For the WoodHive example, technical confirmation requires a
recorded admission, a receipt for the same binding, a successful
status, and matching observed results. A receipt saying
``succeeded'' is insufficient if the published content has the
wrong hash. These checks establish correspondence with the
approved plan; they do not establish that the reported 120 jobs
are factually correct.

For a compound plan, confirmation additionally requires
one attributable observation of the authoritative endpoint
or registry showing all target states under a common commit
identifier. The infrastructure must provide a transactional
update, a staged prepare/commit procedure with simultaneous
visibility, or an atomic switch of the pointer identifying
the current authoritative version. Without such a mechanism,
the system can report partial execution and recovery, but
cannot claim atomic confirmation.

Retries are idempotent: the same binding resumes or returns
the same attempt without producing a second lifecycle effect.
A materially changed attempt requires a new binding and
authorization.

Figure~\ref{fig:lifecycle_binding} separates the coordination
process from execution status. Acceptance can be final while
execution remains pending or fails. A successful receipt
supports technical confirmation only when its observed
result matches the authorized plan. Institutional confirmation
additionally requires the safeguards in
Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}.

\subsection{Hybrid Architecture and Consolidated Requirements}
\label{sec:onchain_offchain}

The architecture places governance records on-chain so that decisions
and their scope can be related to later execution, while keeping graph
processing and storage off-chain. Proposal identifiers, policy references,
participation, decisions, authorizations, hashes, and status records
provide the governance side of that link. RDF content and the services
for validation, provenance, canonicalization, publication, and archival
remain external; an adapter carries out the operation and returns its
receipt. This division is our design choice, rather than an architecture
prescribed by RDF, PROV, or SHACL.

Discussion, evidence review, and voting can also occur
off-chain when their outputs are bound to an attributable
decision record. The design therefore depends on verifiable
connections between components, rather than on placing
every action on a blockchain.

\section{Institutional Extensions and Safeguards for DAOMod}
\label{sec:safeguards}

A technically correct graph operation can still lack institutional
justification. Its authorization may rely on an expired mandate,
its evidence may be subject to disclosure restrictions, or its
decision may result from concentrated authority. Errors also
require correction without erasing the history of earlier
decisions. Addressing RQ3 therefore requires safeguards that
connect the decision and execution records in
Section~\ref{sec:dao_mechanisms} to accountable mandates,
applicable policies, oversight, and recovery.

Section~\ref{sec:safeguard_inputs} explains how we derive
the safeguard requirements: we examine the Coordination
and Execution requirements, identify the institutional risks
they leave unresolved, and specify safeguards to address
those risks. Section~\ref{sec:accountability} explains how actions are
linked to responsible actors and their institutional mandates,
and which records allow an auditor to trace a graph version
from its proposal through approval and execution.
Section~\ref{sec:reversibility} explains how errors are
corrected, earlier graph versions are restored to use,
and emergency interventions are controlled, while preserving
the history of decisions and changes.
Section~\ref{sec:compliance} explains how the design identifies
the policies governing each graph operation and checks whether
the responsible actors have an institutional mandate to
perform it. Whether those policies and mandates satisfy
applicable law requires a separate legal assessment.
Finally, Section~\ref{sec:resilience} explains how the design
limits concentrated control and handles execution failures.
It specifies the checks required before a graph change is
recognized as institutionally authoritative and summarizes
the twelve safeguard requirements (SG1--SG12).

\subsection{Safeguard Derivation and Institutional Risks}
\label{sec:safeguard_inputs}

CR1--CR6 and ER1--ER6 provide the inputs to safeguard derivation.
They specify how eligible participants reach decisions,
how acceptance produces authorization, and how receipts
support technical confirmation. The safeguards add the
institutional conditions governing those activities.

We derive the safeguards using three principles. First,
each safeguard must state what it protects or controls
and what evidence is needed to check whether it is satisfied.
For example, a safeguard requiring valid authority to publish
the WoodHive graph must identify the responsible actor and
the mandate permitting that action.

Second, technical checks support institutional governance
but cannot establish it on their own. A hash can help detect
changed content, and a permission check can establish whether
an account is allowed to perform an operation. Neither check
establishes that the account holder has lawful authority.
Mandates, procedures for challenging decisions, and independent
review therefore require their own records and procedures.

Third, we develop the safeguards for the employment-information
running case. Applying them in another setting requires
a separate assessment of that setting's legal obligations,
organizational responsibilities, and technical infrastructure.

Figure~\ref{fig:accountability} places the safeguards around the
decision-to-execution chain because a failure at any stage can undermine
an otherwise correct operation. Accountability and provenance establish
who is responsible and what evidence supports the action; compliance
rules then constrain its authority, retention, disclosure, and appeal.
Capture resistance addresses concentrated control, while reversibility
and recovery provide a response when errors or incidents occur.
The dashed arrows show connections to the process, not a restriction
of each safeguard to a single stage.

\subsection{Accountability, Provenance, and Auditability}
\label{sec:accountability}

Schwabe et al. connect knowledge graph usage to trust,
privacy, transparency, accountability, and legal governance
\cite{Schwabe2020KGTrustGovernance}. Kassen examines benefits
and risks of blockchain-enabled public-sector decision making
\cite{Kassen2025BlockchainGovernance}. Building on these
concerns, our design requires governance actions to reference
identifiable responsibilities and valid mandates. Control of
an address or signature alone does not satisfy that requirement.

The organizational assignments in
Section~\ref{sec:governance_requirements} therefore require
persistent references to each actor's role, responsible
institution, mandate, and validity period. Sensitive identity
details can remain in a protected registry while governance
records retain resolvable references.

Our decision records identify the transition, evidence,
policy, participants, outcome, rationale, challenges, and
delegation. PROV represents entities, activities, agents,
and derivations \cite{Moreau2013PROV,Lebo2013PROVO}.
We supplement that provenance with records of institutional
authorization.

Equation~\ref{eq:governance_lineage} defines the minimum
lineage for an institutionally confirmed version $v$:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:governance_lineage}
\begin{aligned}
Lineage(v)=\langle&
mandate,proposal,evidence,\\
&decision,authorization,binding,\\
&receipt,priorVersion\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In Equation~\ref{eq:governance_lineage}, $v$ identifies the graph
version and $Lineage(v)$ denotes the linked records needed to
explain its authority and history. The record contains the following
references: $mandate$ identifies the authority to act;
$proposal$ identifies the requested change; $evidence$ identifies
its supporting material; and $decision$ identifies its approval.
The $authorization$ records permission, the $binding$ specifies
execution, and the $receipt$ reports the attempt's result.
The $priorVersion$ identifies the predecessor where one exists.
This is a specification of information to retain and connect,
not a claim that collecting references makes their contents true.

For example, an auditor examining published WoodHive version $v_1$
can follow these references to the curator's proposal, the source
article and validation report, the committee decision, the
publication authorization, the adapter binding, and the receipt.
If $v_1$ is the first version, the prior-version entry records that
no predecessor exists. A recorded signature without a resolvable
institutional mandate leaves the accountability chain incomplete.

Every applicable element must be resolvable and consistent
with the others. Starting from a confirmed version, an
auditor must be able to recover its decision and execution
history. In the other direction, an accepted decision must
lead to its receipt and confirmed consequence or an explicit
pending, failed, expired, or superseded status. This prevents
both unaccountable graph changes and accepted decisions
whose execution disappears from view.

An auditor can reconstruct this history only if records remain
identifiable and interpretable over time. Durable identifiers,
timestamps, signer information, and retention metadata therefore
accompany human-readable exports. Appropriate access controls allow
protected material to be inspected without making every record public,
as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:compliance}. The records are
tamper-evident or append-only under the stated infrastructure assumptions;
they are not claimed to be unconditionally immutable.

These safeguards qualify CR1, CR4, ER1, ER5, and ER6.
The evaluation in Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} can check
whether references and fields are recorded. Establishing
institutional accountability additionally requires the
referenced mandates, identities, rationale, and external
evidence to exist and remain interpretable.

\subsection{Forward-Only Reversibility and Exceptional Governance}
\label{sec:reversibility}

Correction must allow an erroneous graph state to be withdrawn
without deleting the decisions and evidence that produced it.
The design therefore uses forward-only transitions: each
correction or restoration creates a new governance record.

The operation name $\mathit{restore}$ distinguishes reactivation
of a retained version from initial publication, while both can
have published as their target state. It matches the operation
name in the worked model.

For a retained version $v_k$ in state
$s_k\in\{\mathit{superseded},\mathit{deprecated},\mathit{archived}\}$
and the current published version $v_c$, restoration uses:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restoration_plan}
\begin{aligned}
&g_{restore}(v_k,v_c)=\{\\[-1mm]
&\quad(v_k,s_k,\mathit{restore},\mathit{published}),\\
&\quad(v_c,\mathit{published},\mathit{supersede},
\mathit{superseded})\}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:restoration_plan} defines a plan containing two
coordinated changes. The version $v_k$ is the retained version,
$s_k$ is its current state, and $v_c$ is the currently published
version. The subscript $restore$ names the restoration plan;
$k$ and $c$ distinguish the retained and current versions.
Braces collect the two transitions into one set. Each parenthesized
transition lists, in order, the affected version, its source state,
the operation, and its target state. The first republishes $v_k$;
the second supersedes $v_c$. Restoration is confirmed only when the system verifies both
changes together: the retained version becomes authoritative,
and the previously current version becomes superseded.
The infrastructure must make these changes take effect
together, so users do not see two authoritative versions
or an unintended gap between them. If only one change
succeeds, the restoration remains incomplete and requires
recovery, as specified in Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_binding}.

Suppose WoodHive version $v_1$ is retained but superseded, while
$v_2$ is currently published. If review finds the revision in
$v_2$ erroneous and authorizes restoration of $v_1$, the plan
contains two transitions: $v_1$ becomes published again and
$v_2$ becomes superseded. Both versions and their earlier
histories remain available; restoration does not erase $v_2$.

The coupled changes require a new proposal, decision,
authorization, binding, and receipt:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:restoration_records}
\begin{aligned}
Restore(v_k,v_c)=\langle&
p_{new},d_{new},z_{new},\\
&b_{new},r_{new}\rangle,\\
g(p_{new})&=g_{restore}(v_k,v_c).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:restoration_records} groups the new records
required for restoration. $Restore(v_k,v_c)$ denotes this record
set for the retained and current versions. The symbols $p_{new}$,
$d_{new}$, $z_{new}$, $b_{new}$, and $r_{new}$ denote the new
proposal, decision, authorization, binding, and receipt.
The subscript $new$ emphasizes that historical approval is not
reused. The second equality states that the new proposal must contain
the complete restoration plan: return the retained version
$v_k$ to authoritative use and supersede the currently
published version $v_c$. Both changes must be proposed
together.

The requirement to confirm both changes follows
Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_binding}: retained history is useful only
if its return to authoritative use is governed together with withdrawal
of the current version. Dibowski provides a related technical foundation
for recovering past graph versions \cite{Dibowski2024TraceabilityKG}.
Our additional contribution is to require a fresh authorization and
preserve the intervening decision history when such a version is restored.

The design distinguishes four responses:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Ordinary correction} creates and governs
    a new candidate version.

    \item \emph{Suspension} temporarily prevents activation
    or distribution while an issue is investigated.

    \item \emph{Restoration} returns a retained version
    to active use through a new transition plan.

    \item \emph{Emergency intervention} applies a restricted
    procedure when ordinary delay presents unacceptable harm.
\end{itemize}

The proposed emergency policy defines when exceptional
action is permitted, which assets it may affect, and what
the responsible actors may do. It also limits how long
the authority lasts and whether it can be delegated or
renewed. The policy requires notification of the intervention
and an independent review.

For example, the WoodHive model permits a temporary
suspension when an invalid publication is suspected.
This emergency authority cannot be delegated, expires
after ten blocks, and requires notification and independent
review. The intervention must record why it is necessary,
who authorizes and carries it out, and what happens.
We propose these emergency safeguards as part of our
institutional governance design. Their effectiveness
requires evaluation.

Figure~\ref{fig:reversibility} shows how suspension leads
to review and a proposal for correction or restoration.
The resulting operation must still pass the binding and
confirmation checks in Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle_binding}.
If the software responsible for carrying out the operation
fails, or the observed result differs from the approved
plan, the process remains recorded as incomplete and is
referred for review and recovery.

\subsection{Compliance and Legally Bounded Authority}
\label{sec:compliance}

The design represents institutional policies and their
machine-checkable conditions. Determining whether those
policies are legally valid remains the responsibility of
competent institutional and legal authorities.

For transition type $t$, the compliance profile is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:compliance_profile}
\begin{aligned}
\lambda_t=\langle&
jurisdiction,mandate,retention,\\
&disclosure,appeal,hold\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:compliance_profile} describes a policy record,
not a calculation of legal compliance. Here, $\lambda$
names the profile, and the subscript $t$ selects the transition-plan
type to which it applies. Within the record, $jurisdiction$
identifies the applicable legal context; $mandate$ identifies the
institutional authority; $retention$ identifies preservation rules;
$disclosure$ identifies access and release restrictions;
$appeal$ identifies the contestation procedure; and $hold$
identifies applicable restrictions on execution or preservation.
These fields reference obligations and procedures whose validity
requires assessment by the responsible authorities.

For illustration, suppose the policy for publishing the WoodHive
graph requires a current institutional mandate, restricted access
to supporting evidence, and preservation of records during an
appeal. The profile identifies those rules and their authoritative
sources. If an appeal creates an execution hold, then the publication must
wait even when the graph passes technical validation. This example
illustrates a proposed policy, not a finding about applicable law.

The compliance profile defined in
Equation~\ref{eq:compliance_profile} identifies the policies
and obligations that the system must accommodate. These
requirements affect how the system stores information,
grants authority, and preserves records.

An actor cannot delegate authority beyond the scope of
their own institutional mandate. Protected evidence and
personal information require controlled storage and access;
public governance records may contain references or hashes
instead of disclosing the underlying information.

When records are exported or moved to another system,
their identifiers and the information needed to verify
their integrity must be preserved. Similarly, withdrawing
a graph version from active use does not permit its deletion
if an applicable retention rule or preservation hold
requires it to be kept.

Atzori argues that blockchain-based decentralization does not
remove the need for institutional coordination
\cite{Atzori2017BlockchainGovernance}.
In our design, appeals and external legal or administrative
decisions can suspend execution or require corrective proposals.
Their effects use the authorization, binding, and receipt
chain, with authority derived from the applicable mandate.
This mechanism is a proposed institutional extension.

Evaluation can check whether policy references, holds,
disclosure classifications, and authorized interventions
are represented and enforced. Legal adequacy requires
jurisdiction-specific assessment beyond contract tests.

\subsection{Capture Resistance and Governance-Failure Safeguards}
\label{sec:resilience}

DAO research identifies governance vulnerabilities associated
with concentrated token ownership \cite{Han2025DAOReview}
and participation and cross-chain risks in metagovernance
\cite{Weidener2026Metagovernance}.
For the design in Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}
and \ref{sec:dao_mechanisms}, we extend the threat inventory
to incompatible assignments, delegation abuse, censorship,
deadlock, emergency-power abuse, compromised adapters,
and selective non-execution. This is our analysis of the
proposed architecture.

The design addresses governance threats by checking who
holds authority and how that authority is exercised.
Before an actor participates, assignment checks identify
incompatible responsibilities. Committee membership and
institutional mandates also establish whether participants
represent independent principals: several accounts controlled
by one institution do not count as several independent
authorities. When authority is delegated, the delegation
must have a defined scope and validity period and remain
available for inspection. Records of challenges, dissent,
and proposal progress then help reviewers identify decisions
that are disputed or deliberately obstructed.

The same need for accountability continues after a decision.
If too few eligible participants contribute, an executor
does not act within the permitted time, or an execution
receipt never arrives, the process must record the unresolved
outcome and refer it for review or a new proposal. For
example, approval to publish the WoodHive graph does not
establish that publication occurs. If publication remains
unconfirmed, records identifying the execution service,
its software version, and the attempted operation support
investigation and recovery. Any emergency response must
remain within the limits specified in
Section~\ref{sec:reversibility}.

For each material threat $f$, the design records:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:threat_coverage}
\begin{aligned}
Coverage(f)=\langle&
Prevent_f,Detect_f,\\
&Recover_f,Residual_f\rangle.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

In Equation~\ref{eq:threat_coverage}, $f$ denotes a material threat,
and $Coverage(f)$ collects the response specified for that threat.
$Prevent_f$ identifies measures intended to prevent it;
$Detect_f$ identifies how its occurrence is noticed;
$Recover_f$ identifies the response after detection; and
$Residual_f$ records the risk that remains. The subscript $f$
connects each component to the same threat. The equation does not
calculate a probability or demonstrate that the measures are effective.

Suppose the committee approves publication of the WoodHive
graph, but the software responsible for publishing it does
not complete the operation. Before execution begins, checking
that the appointed publication service is available can reduce
the likelihood of this failure.

If no execution receipt arrives within the specified time,
the system records that publication remains unconfirmed.
A missing receipt does not establish whether publication
fails or whether the service publishes the graph but fails
to report the result. The incident therefore requires
investigation before a newly authorized attempt proceeds.

These procedures help identify and respond to incomplete
execution, but they cannot guarantee that the external
publication service remains available. That continuing
dependency is the residual risk. Specifying the procedures
does not establish how effectively they manage that risk
in practice.

\paragraph{Institutional Confirmation.}

Technical confirmation in Equation~\ref{eq:technical_confirmation}
establishes that the observed result matches the authorized
plan. Institutional confirmation additionally requires the
applicable modeled safeguards.

Let $\Gamma_{conf}(b)$ be the complete set of safeguards
required by the transition, compliance, distribution, and
exceptional-status policies. Let $PolicyResolved(b)$ mean
that these policies and checks are identifiable, and let
$Passed(g_s,b,r)$ mean that safeguard $g_s$ has a recorded
satisfactory result. The safeguard gate is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:safeguard_gate}
\begin{aligned}
SafeguardGate(b,r)\iff{}&
PolicyResolved(b)\\
&\land\forall g_s\in\Gamma_{conf}(b):\\
&\qquad Passed(g_s,b,r).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:safeguard_gate} checks binding $b$ together with
receipt $r$. $PolicyResolved(b)$ means that the applicable policies
and required checks are identified. The set $\Gamma_{conf}(b)$
contains all safeguards required for confirmation of this binding;
$conf$ abbreviates confirmation. The expression
$\forall g_s\in\Gamma_{conf}(b)$ means ``for every safeguard
$g_s$ in this set.'' Here, $g_s$ denotes a safeguard, not a
transition plan. $Passed(g_s,b,r)$ requires a recorded satisfactory
result for that safeguard in the context of the binding and receipt.
Every required check must pass;
an unresolved check cannot be silently omitted from the set.

For a WoodHive publication, the applicable checks might require
a valid institutional mandate, participation by independent
principals, permitted evidence access, and no unresolved hold.
The gate passes only when the applicable policies are identified
and every required check has a recorded satisfactory result.
An unresolved mandate check cannot be treated as a pass simply
because the adapter returns the correct graph hash.

The institutional confirmation condition is:
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:institutional_confirmation}
\begin{aligned}
&InstitutionallyConfirmed(b,r)\\
&\quad\iff TechnicalConfirmed(b,r)\\
&\qquad\land SafeguardGate(b,r).
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:institutional_confirmation} combines two necessary
checks for the same binding $b$ and receipt $r$.
$TechnicalConfirmed(b,r)$ requires the observed execution result
to match the authorized plan. $SafeguardGate(b,r)$ requires the
applicable institutional checks to pass. Institutional
confirmation requires both. Neither a correct technical
result with unresolved safeguards nor completed safeguards with
a failed operation is sufficient.

In a deployment following the proposed design, the WoodHive adapter
would place approved content in a staging location and return a
receipt for checking technical confirmation. If a required mandate check remains
unresolved, institutional confirmation still fails and the staged
graph does not become authoritative. Only when both conditions
hold does the design recognize the transition as institutionally
confirmed. This modeled status is not a legal certification. The worked
confirmation's atomic-update flag expresses an intended property;
structural validation of that flag does not demonstrate atomic
execution. The supplemental local publication test does not execute
the complete institutional safeguard gate or compound restoration.

Some safeguards must be checked before an execution attempt
is allowed to begin. The system checks that the responsible
actors have valid institutional mandates, no applicable
policy hold blocks the operation, and access to the required
evidence is permitted. It records these checks with the
decision to admit the execution attempt.

For a critical change, the system also checks that authority
is distributed as required. Here, $t=type(g(p))$ identifies
the plan type of proposal $p$. The condition $Distributed(t)$
checks the organizational allocation of authority, while
$DecentralizedDecision(p,\kappa_t)$ checks the participants
and decision weight actually counted for that proposal.

For example, before publishing the WoodHive graph, these
checks establish whether the approved operation may proceed.
They cannot yet establish whether the correct graph is
published. Checks that depend on the result therefore take
place after the attempt, using the execution receipt and
its supporting evidence.

Only an institutionally confirmed plan establishes the
authoritative lifecycle state. Publication is therefore
staged until the applicable gate passes. A technically
correct staged result can remain non-authoritative when
a required institutional check is unresolved.

Continuing monitoring and mandatory post-hoc review remain
explicit obligations. They can trigger suspension or a new
corrective transition and are not treated as completed
merely because confirmation occurs. The status
$InstitutionallyConfirmed$ means satisfaction of the modeled
policy, rather than proof of legal validity.

Listing~\ref{lst:safeguard_gate} summarizes the checks across
admission and confirmation. Its early conditions are applied
before execution; receipt-dependent conditions are applied
after the attempt. The checks can be implemented through
contracts, identity services, protected repositories,
adapters, and human review procedures.

\begin{lstlisting}[
caption={Platform-independent institutional safeguard gate},
label={lst:safeguard_gate},
numbers=left,
numberstyle=\tiny,
stepnumber=1,
numbersep=5pt
]
applySafeguards(proposal, decision, binding, receipt):
    require accountable_mandate(proposal.initiator)
    require applicable_compliance_profile(type(proposal.g))
    require evidence_access_and_retention_permitted()
    require separation_and_delegation_constraints_hold()
    require structural_and_realized_distribution_hold()

        schedule_independent_review()

    require lineage_complete(proposal, decision, binding)

    if receipt.missing or receipt.failed:
        record_and_escalate_execution_failure()
        return NOT_CONFIRMED

    require lineage_complete(binding, receipt, resulting_version)
    monitor_concentration_and_non_execution()
    return MODELED_SAFEGUARD_GATE_SATISFIED
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Evaluation}
\label{sec:evaluation}

The proposed design describes how knowledge graph governance
should work. Evaluation asks which parts can be represented and
implemented, and what the resulting evidence establishes.
We first inspect the editable models and compare the original
prototype with the requirements. We then examine two bounded
publication demonstrations: the supplemental episode tests the

For example, a committee may approve publication of the
WoodHive graph and record that approval on the blockchain.
This record alone does not show that the publication service
stores the approved graph correctly. The evaluation therefore
distinguishes evidence of approval from evidence that the
approved operation is carried out.

This section
assesses implementation support for the design in
Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}--\ref{sec:safeguards}
and identifies the remaining gaps. It also checks whether the
institutional additions are represented in editable, linked
model artifacts.

\subsection{Evaluation Objects and Evidence Boundaries}
\label{sec:eval_boundary}

Following design science research \cite{hevner2004design}, we assess
whether the constructed artifact addresses the requirements of its
intended context. This requires separating three evaluation objects.
OLR1--OLR12, CR1--CR6, ER1--ER6, and SG1--SG12 state what the
\emph{design specification} requires. The integrated institutional
DAOMod ontology, linked DAO-ML and ET-DM worked models, and requirement
mappings make those requirements inspectable as \emph{model artifacts}.
The smart-contract prototype and tests provide the \emph{implementation}
evidence. Model checks and execution tests therefore answer different
questions and cannot substitute for one another.

We first evaluate the original smart-contract prototype,
which we call the baseline. We compare it with all 36
requirements, including those it does not implement.
Its tests examine role assignments, permissions, recorded
lifecycle changes, and collective decisions.

The baseline tests operate within the blockchain software. They do
not run extraction, external RDF validation, graph storage, publication,
or external provenance services, so they cannot establish the full design in
Sections~\ref{sec:smart_contracts}--\ref{sec:safeguards}. For example,
recording approval of WoodHive publication supplies no receipt showing
where the graph is stored or whether its content matches the approved version.

The two further demonstrations in Section~\ref{sec:publication_episode}
address this missing connection in a local setting. The earlier episode
starts from hand-configured contracts. The new proof of concept additionally
reads the worked WoodHive model and links selected ontology/view bindings
to deployment parameters, execution checks, and recorded results.
Both use synthetic RDF and local accounts. Neither constitutes institutional
deployment, and neither changes the baseline classifications or coverage.
The WoodHive case in Section~\ref{sec:runningcase} supplies the meaning
of the operations, not an empirical extraction dataset.

\subsection{Evaluation Design and Scope}
\label{sec:eval_method}

Within the boundaries in Section~\ref{sec:eval_boundary}, we combine source inspection, automated tests, instrumented coverage, and requirement-level mapping. The evaluated components are the role and permission registry, semantic-asset lifecycle registry, collective-decision contract, and optimistic-governance contract. The baseline revision is \texttt{3c55c6903fe6fb38177889e22adf9639f8b9cda5}.\footnote{Baseline reviewer entry point: \url{https://github.com/alexbafana/MKM-POL21-2025/tree/3c55c6903fe6fb38177889e22adf9639f8b9cda5/reproducibility/paper-b}.}

Execution checks start by compiling the contracts and deploying repeatable
fixtures, then run the tests, instrument Solidity coverage, and validate
the manifest and requirement crosswalk. The two decision procedures
exercise different routes to execution: the committee procedure requires
affirmative voting, whereas the optimistic procedure allows execution
after a challenge period unless an eligible participant objects.

The evaluated contract names its registry states \emph{Created}, \emph{Validated}, \emph{Approved}, and \emph{Published}. These names are retained when reporting implementation behavior. They differ from the design model in Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}, where a created version is a candidate and authorization is deliberately modeled as a decision status rather than as an asset state. The evaluation makes this mismatch explicit because an ``Approved'' registry value can otherwise blur the distinction between institutional authorization and a semantic-asset lifecycle state.

Four evaluation questions (EQ1--EQ4) make the inference from evidence to findings
explicit. Table~\ref{tab:evaluation_questions} states both the evidence used
and the limit of each answer. EQ1 asks for systematic design-to-implementation
traceability; EQ2 asks whether the represented controls behave correctly on
the tested paths; EQ3 asks whether the executed evidence is broad enough to
expose untested contract structure and can be rerun from a clean checkout; and
EQ4 asks which claims remain unsupported despite successful execution.

Five situations connect the running case to the assessment. S1 asks
whether a validated graph can receive approval without treating that
approval as publication. S2 then follows the registry sequence from
submission through validation and approval to publication. These use
the implementation's approved state, whose difference from the design
model is explained above.

S3 examines reprocessing by registering a further version under a
changed model version. Whether the new content corrects an extraction
error remains outside the on-chain boundary. S4 asks whether a previously
published version can be restored, and S5 asks whether a proposal that
misses quorum reaches a terminal outcome when its voting period ends.
Together, the situations sample successful and unsuccessful paths through
the lifecycle in Section~\ref{sec:lifecycle}, because each depends on
an authorization decision the contracts are intended to govern.

This selection does not cover every concern in
Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}--\ref{sec:safeguards}.
Deprecation and archival have no corresponding transition in the evaluated
build, and it implements neither an exceptional transition nor the policy
required by SG5--SG6. Capture, collusion, and trust erosion also require
assessment of institutional relationships; this evaluation includes no
independent organizations with which to assess them. End-to-end publication
is outside the baseline boundary in Section~\ref{sec:eval_boundary}.
The situations therefore illustrate selected governance behavior, while
the requirement-level mapping in Section~\ref{sec:capability_assessment}
accounts for every design requirement, including those left unimplemented.

EQ1 treats a complete platform-independent requirement as the unit of
analysis, rather than equating a passing test with a satisfied requirement.
For each requirement $q$, the authors first examine its clauses and locate
candidate state, functions, guards, events, and tests. Positive, negative,
boundary, and bypass paths are then inspected to determine which clauses
the implementation supports. The most conservative supported status is
recorded with its evidence paths and unmet clauses, and identifier
uniqueness and aggregate counts are checked mechanically.
The classification rule uses requirement $q$ as its input:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:coverage_status}
s(q)=
\begin{cases}
\mathrm{E}, & C_b(q)\neq\emptyset\ \land\ H(q)=C_b(q)\ \land\ D(q),\\
\mathrm{N}, & H(q)=\emptyset,\\
\mathrm{P}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}

Equation~\ref{eq:coverage_status} is a classification rule applied
to each requirement $q$; $s(q)$ is its assigned status. Here, $q$
denotes a requirement; $r$ remains reserved for execution receipts.
$C_b(q)$ contains the requirement's clauses observable within the
baseline contract boundary, and $H(q)$ contains those clauses with
a reachable implementation representation or enforcement point.
The subscript $b$ identifies that boundary. $D(q)$ means execution
evidence distinguishes the intended behavior from a relevant
failure where applicable. The symbols $\emptyset$, $\neq$, and $=$
mean ``empty set,'' ``not equal,'' and ``equal,'' respectively. The first row assigns E only when the observable clause
set is nonempty, every such clause is represented, and the required
execution distinction is demonstrated. The second assigns N when
no clause has a reachable implementation. All other cases receive P.
For example, recording a WoodHive publication approval without the
required link to its execution result cannot establish full support
for a requirement demanding both. This illustrates the coding rule;
the reported classifications follow the actual evidence crosswalk.
Thus, E denotes directly exercised support for all contract-observable clauses,
P denotes at least one represented clause but a missing clause, binding,
scope, or distinguishing execution, and N denotes no corresponding reachable
implementation. The rule deliberately biases ambiguous cases toward P or N.
It does not turn an E into evidence of legal adequacy. The coding is an
auditable authorial interpretation; no independent second coder or inter-rater
agreement is claimed.

The assessment translates broad governance goals into observable conditions.
For \emph{authorization enforceability}, an authorized caller must be able
to complete a guarded transition while an unauthorized caller is rejected
without a state change. Attribution of the acting party supports
\emph{accountability}; a durable record of the decision, actor, and asset
supports \emph{auditability}. Those records must also allow the transition
sequence to be reconstructed for \emph{lifecycle traceability}.

Recovery and failure require separate checks. \emph{Reversibility} asks
whether authorization can restore a published state; restoration is
specified but unimplemented in the evaluated build. \emph{Termination
under quorum failure} asks whether a proposal that misses quorum reaches
a terminal state from which it cannot execute. This is narrower than
Section~\ref{sec:resilience}, which also requires escalation of a stalled
procedure. That escalation mechanism is absent from the evaluated build
and is not reported as exercised.

The same distinction bounds the assessment of compliance. Contract
execution can expose whether policy references, protected-evidence metadata,
exceptional policies, and review hooks are represented, but it cannot
establish legal adequacy. Whether an institution meets retention, disclosure,
and appeal obligations requires a structured requirements walkthrough or
legal review. Section~\ref{sec:compliance} specifies those obligations
at design level; the technical assessment of RQ3 concerns attribution,
traceability, forward-only history, and failure handling.

Evidence is drawn from four sources and supports claims of different
strength. Contract sources establish \emph{existence and reachability}: which
state, authorization checks, and guards are present. Executed tests establish
\emph{observed behavior} under their constructed conditions. Instrumented
coverage establishes \emph{structural breadth} within the declared source
boundary, not conformance. Finally, the machine-readable crosswalk exposes the
\emph{authorial interpretation} connecting that technical evidence to each
platform-independent requirement. A higher source-coverage percentage cannot
upgrade P or N to E, and a passing test named after a requirement cannot do so
unless all contract-observable clauses meet the rule in
Equation~\ref{eq:coverage_status}.

These sources provide no evidence of \emph{institutional suitability}:
neither legal compliance, nor organizational legitimacy of the modeled roles
and decision rights, nor operational suitability under institutional workload
follows from source inspection or test execution. The test suites and
crosswalk were produced by the authors alongside the artifact, so they reflect
the properties the authors set out to demonstrate and cannot rule out untested
failure modes or interpretation bias. These limits apply especially to RQ3,
for which the evaluation can inspect event-level attribution and traceability
but cannot establish legal compliance or institutional legitimacy.

\paragraph{Explicit Institutional Model Views.}

The base models retain their upstream schemas, so four additional views
make the institutional additions visible rather than leaving them in
external metadata. The Organization view exposes the governed assets,
lifecycle, scoped permissions, assignments, policy links, and distribution
constraints. The Coordination view uses that authority to specify exact
plans, admission, decision policies, validation prerequisites, outcomes,
and configuration proposals. The Execution view connects the decision
to scoped authorization, guards, content identity, receipts, and confirmation,
while the Safeguard view exposes the twelve institutional conditions.
Each view has editable XML, an editable diagram, and an SVG rendering
under \texttt{extended-views/}.

Across these views, 36 explicit concept bindings carry the
ontology class URI, requirement identifier, upstream model
element, worked-instance field pointer, and concrete value.
Five ontology properties connect the views: a plan has an
asset and requires a policy; an authorization has a plan;
a confirmation has a receipt; and admission has a mandate.
A separate binding diagram makes those links visible.
Appendices~\ref{app:dao_ml_notation} and
\ref{app:etdm_notation} define its visual symbols and connection
rules; Appendix~\ref{app:woodhive_models} applies them to WoodHive.
This is a proposed institutional modeling notation defined
by \texttt{InstitutionalModelViews.xsd}. Compatibility with
the original DAO-ML or ET-DM editors is not claimed.

The supplied walkthrough shows how to define an asset and
its organizational scopes, specify a collective decision,
bind its authorization to execution evidence, and add the
applicable institutional review conditions. Modelers edit
the worked instance and profile, regenerate the concrete
views, and check their bindings before assessing semantic
adequacy. For example, publication uses a version-specific
plan and receipt rather than a generic write transaction;
restoration requires a fresh plan while retaining earlier
history. This demonstrates how the proposed vocabulary is
used, without reporting an independently assessed usability
study.

\paragraph{Requirement Mapping and Worked Traces.}

To check whether every requirement has an editable representation,
the mapping covers OLR1--OLR12, CR1--CR6, ER1--ER6, and SG1--SG12.
Each row connects the requirement wording to an institutional concept,
a resolvable base-model element, and a field in the worked instance;
reverse bindings allow the same connection to be followed back from
the profile. This establishes mechanical traceability. Whether a
concept adequately addresses every clause of its requirement still
requires semantic assessment.

The publication example makes this traceability concrete by following
one version and its evidence manifest through proposal, acceptance,
authorization, execution binding, receipt, and confirmation. Keeping
the asset state separate from decision and execution status prevents
approval from being mistaken for completed publication. The restoration
example adds the coordinated reactivation of a retained version and
supersession of the current one under fresh authorization and a common
commit. Other traces illustrate what happens when a proposal is rejected
or challenged, an authorization expires, a receipt is missing, or an
exceptional suspension requires notification and review. All these traces
are synthetic design examples; their principals, mandates, digests,
thresholds, and review records are illustrative, not additional
institutional or contract executions.

\paragraph{Validation Procedure and Results.}

The model validator first checks compatibility by validating both XML
files against the pinned, unmodified upstream schemas. It then resolves
model references and the 36 requirement/instance bindings and checks
the worked publication and restoration specifications. Both files pass
schema validation and all mappings resolve.

Deliberately invalid variants check whether the validator detects violations
of its own conditions. Seventeen variants are rejected, covering wrong
asset or plan references, failed or self-validation, insufficient quorum,
expired mandates or authorization, wrong executors, content mismatches,
broken lineage, protected-evidence exposure, unbounded exceptional authority,
and incomplete restoration. These results show detection of the selected
modeled violations; they do not establish deployed safeguard enforcement.

The integrated ontology requires a different check: adding institutional
concepts must preserve the original graph. A separate RDF validator
therefore compares the integrated ontology with the complete union of
the original and extension, allowing for renamed blank nodes. It confirms
that all 3,017 original triples remain within the 3,162-triple integrated
graph and that the original file's checksum is unchanged.

The validator also checks the extension's declared structure: 36 institutional
classes and their requirement links, five object properties, three
institutional existential restrictions, and valid upstream subclass targets.
It checks the absence of imports and preservation through an RDF/XML round
trip. Removing an original axiom or institutional class, or adding an
unresolved import, causes each of three altered graphs to be rejected.
The integrated file can consequently be rebuilt and checked from the two
source snapshots, without claiming an OWL logical-consistency proof.

The views must agree with both the ontology and the instance they display.
The view validator therefore checks the four documents against the proposed
schema, resolves all 36 class/requirement/base-element bindings, and compares
their concrete values with the authoritative worked instance. It also
checks five property links against the ontology's domains and ranges.
Six variants test these connections by removing a concept or relationship,
using an unknown class or wrong requirement, changing a value, or leaving
a dangling target; all six are rejected. This establishes selected
representation checks, not acceptance by upstream editors or independent
evidence of usefulness.

The notation validator checks the 36-symbol catalog, nine
distinct reserved IKG markers, paired-tab frame metadata, and
36 exported diagram bindings. Appendix~\ref{app:dao_ml_notation}
records the manual comparison with the thesis core notation tables. Two invalid
catalog variants are rejected. These are catalog and metadata
checks, not a usability or perceptual evaluation.

The scripts and pinned RDF-parser dependencies are supplied;
GitHub Actions runs the same automated checks from a fresh checkout
and reports success at the model revision.

\url{https://github.com/alexbafana/MKM-POL21-2025/actions/runs/38600502994}.}
This automated execution is not an independent expert
assessment. No OWL-DL consistency, class-satisfiability,
conservative-extension, RDF-canonicalization conformance,
legal-validity, or production-enforcement result is claimed.
The model checks do not change the baseline 2 E, 16 P, and
18 N classifications, the 180 baseline tests, or the separate
22-test publication episode.

Table~\ref{tab:coverage} records seventeen legacy implementation requirements as exercised and
five as specified without a corresponding implementation. The design-level
crosswalk gives a different and more relevant result: of the 36 requirements
derived in Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}--\ref{sec:safeguards},
two have direct exercised support, sixteen have partial support, and eighteen

design requirements specify cross-layer bindings and institutional safeguards
that the earlier implementation suite was not constructed to test. The suite
comprises 180 tests across eight files, with no focused or skipped tests; all
pass in the clean reproduction at the cited revision. Over the explicitly
declared contract boundary, instrumented coverage is 80.12\% (137/171)
statements, 64.89\% (122/188) branches, 73.61\% (53/72) functions, and
79.49\% (186/234) lines. These measurements expose residual unexecuted code
and support EQ3, but neither test quantity nor coverage percentage is treated
as a quality or conformance score.

Authorization enforceability is exercised primarily through negative
paths that cover unauthorized callers, invalid lifecycle orderings, temporal
and objection constraints, invalid arguments, and attempted bypasses. The
corresponding positive paths prevent a rejected call alone from being mistaken
for evidence that the intended caller can complete the operation. A permission
bit is a numbered Boolean flag that enables a particular action. The tests,
for example, establish that granting bit 28 to a role confers proposal rights
on every holder of that role, while revoking bit 29 withdraws objection rights
from that role and leaves them intact for a role that retains the bit.

Accountability and lifecycle traceability rest on the events emitted by
the lifecycle registry. Each governed transition records the address of
the acting party, and where a transition is executed by a governance
contract the recorded address is that contract rather than the account
that submitted the transaction. The stored record of a semantic asset
preserves its submitter, submission time, content hash and version, and
successive versions of one dataset remain individually addressable, so
the sequence of transitions applied to a dataset can be reconstructed
from on-chain state.

What this establishes is attribution of technical execution: a
transition can be traced to the governance mechanism that carried it
out. Two further links are required for the institutional
accountability described in Section~\ref{sec:accountability}, and they
are of different kinds. The binding between an account and a legal
person belongs to an identity layer that this architecture treats as
external: the artifact records addresses and stores an attestation at
onboarding, but verifying that attestation is the responsibility of the
credential infrastructure rather than of the governance contracts. The
binding between a transition and the deliberation behind it is, by
contrast, within scope and incomplete: the individual votes are recorded
by the governance contracts as their own events, but the registry record
carries no reference to the proposal that authorized the transition, so
the two event streams cannot be related without off-chain correlation.

The ordering guarantee controls progress through the registry but does
not protect the whole lifecycle. A graph must pass validation before
approval and receive approval before publication; repeated approval and
publication are idempotent. However, validation evidence can still be
rewritten afterward, and no operation withdraws or supersedes a published
state. Nor does the registry enforce the order in which the two governance
bodies act. The guaranteed forward edges therefore leave evidence integrity,
replacement, and coordination-order gaps.

Negative tests show why these guarantees are insufficient for the design
requirements. Approval and publication check the syntactic validation
result alone, allowing a semantically failed graph to progress. Because
one role can submit, validate, and approve, role permissions do not enforce
separation of duties. The stored publication identifier is not verified
against the content hash and can be reused for several graphs; publishing
a new version also leaves its predecessor published.

Other tests expose gaps in participant authority and collective approval.
The onboarding attestation expires but is not checked at document submission,
and a zero consortium quorum allows one vote to make a proposal successful.
These are executed counterexamples, rather than concerns inferred solely
from source code. They identify where the baseline needs revision or
completion before stronger governance claims can be assessed.

\subsection{Local Publication Episodes and Feasibility Proof of Concept}
\label{sec:publication_episode}

The baseline identifies a central RQ2 gap: a collective decision can cause a
registry change but is not necessary for it, and no receipt verifies the
corresponding off-chain publication. To test a concrete repair, a separate
artificial integration episode implements one single-call publication profile
in \texttt{BoundPublication.sol}, using the existing
\texttt{ValidationCommittee} and voting token. This episode does not replace
the baseline implementation, upgrade its 36 requirement classifications, or
attribute new code to the earlier evaluated revision. The source, synthetic
RDF fixture, adapter, tests, results, and review forms are under
\texttt{reproducibility/paper-b/publication/} in the supplemental revision.\footnote{Supplemental reviewer entry point:
\url{https://github.com/alexbafana/MKM-POL21-2025/tree/29032b2174fd0505b79a95c275e6f442747c17d7/reproducibility/paper-b/publication}. The supplemental source and clean reproduction evidence are pinned to commit \texttt{29032b2174fd0505b79a95c275e6f442747c17d7}.}

The unit of analysis is one exact publication authorization for an immutable
dataset/version record. Its content digest is Keccak-256 over UTF-8 N-Quads
canonicalized using RDFC-1.0 through the lockfile-pinned
\texttt{rdf-canonize} version 5.0.0. The synthetic employment fixture is not an
extraction dataset or a prediction about WoodHive. A narrow executable rule
requires parseable N-Quads, one permitted employment classification literal,
and a source IRI. This supplies a transparent test prerequisite rather than
an estimate of NLP, EMTAK, or general semantic-validation accuracy.

The executed chain has five steps. First, a producer submits a stable
chain/contract/dataset/version identifier, content digest, and model digest;
the version cannot be overwritten. Second, a distinct validator submits both
syntax and semantic outcomes plus a validation-evidence digest. Both outcomes
must pass, and validation cannot be rewritten after the version has progressed.
Third, the committee votes on the exact authorization call. Three local voters
have equal weight and a 34\% quorum; two votes succeed while one is insufficient.
The authorization fixes the asset, expiry, and exact proposal identifier.
The receiving contract checks that the derived single-call proposal is in the
Governor's Executed state during its callback. A direct permission holder
cannot authorize, and an accepted relay-wrapped call cannot substitute for that
exact proposal.

Fourth, the appointed adapter checks the authorization, expiry, and submitted
content before writing a content-addressed object to a local filesystem RDF
store. It then reads back and canonicalizes the object and compares its digest
with the approved digest. Fifth, the adapter submits the proposal identifier,
observed digest, publication identifier, and receipt digest. The registry
confirms only the authorized version and matching content, records the receipt,
and emits the version, decision, publication identifier, receipt digest, and
adapter address. The saved JSON manifest also identifies the chain, registry,
model/evidence digests, and confirmation transaction. An offline verifier
recomputes the object and receipt digests; the execution test independently
asserts the corresponding registry state and event arguments. Offline
verification is an integrity check, not an independent replay of blockchain
history. Table~\ref{tab:publication_episode} summarizes the checks
performed in the supplemental episode and the limitations
of the resulting evidence.

The supplemental episode shows a decision-to-publication connection, but
its hand-configured parameters do not show how the modeling contribution
informs implementation. We therefore add a separate proof of concept for

model elements can configure an executable process and remain traceable
to its result, rather than to implement the complete governance design.
The code, fixture, mappings, results, and reproduction instructions are
published at a pinned revision.\footnote{WoodHive feasibility prototype
and recorded evidence:
\url{https://github.com/alexbafana/MKM-POL21-2025/tree/fb414784d9516ebd979a7b8783fabb464dfb9b9d/reproducibility/paper-b/feasibility}.

\texttt{cd046a9d492353b39e33526eb45e095d05dce8f9}.

\url{https://github.com/alexbafana/MKM-POL21-2025/actions/runs/40752837216}.}

A profile-specific adapter reads the existing worked JSON instance and
checks 14 selected requirement bindings against the integrated ontology,
base models, and explicit XML views. It derives commitments to the source
models, plan, and policy, then configures producer/executor A, validator B,
and committee B/C. A narrow extension of the existing committee requires all snapshot
voting weight to vote in favor, so abstention cannot satisfy unanimity.
With two equal voting allocations and 100\% quorum, this realizes the
model's two-principal policy. Voting lasts 30 blocks and execution
waits five further blocks. Mandate intervals are translated into
block offsets from deployment, and the illustrative expiry value 100 becomes
a 100-block upper bound for the authorization window. These translations
are explicit prototype conventions. This is a hand-written adapter for a
bounded profile, not general ontology execution or automatic generation
of arbitrary DAO-ML or ET-DM contracts.

The new \texttt{ModelBoundPublication.sol} contract requires the exact
executed committee call and matching plan and validation-evidence commitments.
It checks appointed actors and mandate intervals, expiry, and the execution
delay; admission reserves the authorization before publication. The adapter

actual SHA-256 digest, writes a local object, and verifies its read-back.
The receipt connects the model commitments, decision, evidence, graph,
and executor to confirmation. These measured digests replace placeholders

\subsection{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

The evaluation supports selected mechanisms while identifying the distance between the design and institutional deployment. For RQ1, role-level guards and identifiable versions provide only part of the scoped authority and organizational constraints in Section~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}. For RQ2, the baseline permits direct approval without a collective decision. The supplemental profile repairs exact binding for one local publication, under trusted-service assumptions. The model-linked proof of concept additionally configures selected publication checks from the worked model and records their execution; it does not validate the complete institutional design. For RQ3, technical attribution and history do not establish the mandate, compliance, review, and capture safeguards in Section~\ref{sec:safeguards}.

\subsubsection{Relation of the Findings to Prior Work}

The findings reinforce the distinction between preserving data history
and governing its use. PROV represents production and derivation
\cite{Moreau2013PROV}; lifecycle research organizes the recurrent work
of integration, updates, querying, and provenance
\cite{Geisler2025KGLifecycle}; and Dibowski supports change tracking
and recovery of prior graph versions \cite{Dibowski2024TraceabilityKG}.
Our baseline gaps motivate connecting that history to collective
authorization and an observed operation result. This is our inference
from the implementation evidence. KG.gov's data-governance framework
\cite{MeronoPenuela2025KGgov} and research on human oversight in graph
construction \cite{Zhang2025TrustworthyKG} provide related context.

Han et al. review DAO proposals, voting, and concentrated-owner
vulnerabilities \cite{Han2025DAOReview}. Riabokon compares
institutional governance arrangements in RARI, Arbitrum,
and Optimism \cite{Riabokon2026ProgrammableGovernance}.
Zukowski distinguishes current governance concentration
from launch allocations and holder counts
\cite{Zukowski2026GovernanceConcentration}.
Our results similarly require assessment of effective control,
rather than mechanism presence alone. OLR12 and SG10--SG11
are our extension of that concern to institutional lifecycle
rights, not a tested result of those studies.

Our specialization uses model-driven foundations to express this connection.
DAO-ML provides the organizational constructs \cite{Avanzo2024DAOML},
and ET-DM extends T-DM for token economies \cite{Avanzo2025ETDM}.
The organizational focus of the 2025 DAOMod article
\cite{Avanzo2025DAOMod} and subsequent work on token economies and voting
\cite{Avanzo2026DAOModCoordination} form part of that foundation;
Avanzo's thesis supplies the integrated layer model
\cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}. We specialize the connections across those layers
for graph versions, execution results, and institutional mandates.
Binding and receipt requirements address the difficulty of tracing
otherwise obscured decision points, a concern also identified in
metagovernance research \cite{Weidener2026Metagovernance}.

\subsubsection{Threats to Validity}

\emph{Construct validity.} The observable contracts and events record
technical actions, so they cannot by themselves establish legitimate
authority or adequate institutional audit evidence. The same limit applies
to the models: schema compatibility and graph-union preservation in
Section~\ref{sec:model_artifact_validation} do not demonstrate complete
semantics or OWL consistency. Both the E/P/N crosswalk and the meaning
of requirement-to-model links therefore still need independent assessment.
The objection-window result is also bounded by inspected and tested paths,
rather than formal verification; a changed executor or added timelock
would require reassessment.

\emph{Internal validity.} Clean builds, pinned dependencies, and manifest
checks reduce the chance of reporting stale artifacts, while positive and
negative paths and coverage expose selective-execution gaps. They cannot
exclude untested behavior: branch coverage is incomplete and the authors
construct the tests alongside the artifact. Interpretation also depends
on deployment settings, including the thirty-block committee voting period
and zero consortium quorum, and on the trusted validator and adapter in
the supplemental episode.

\emph{External validity.} All three implementation evaluations use local accounts controlled by one party. No independent institution participates in a decision, and no graph is published to a production decentralized knowledge graph. The running case supports derivation rather than operational deployment. Divergent interests, institutional workloads, long-term availability, and other legal settings remain unevaluated.

\emph{Conclusion validity.} Immutable revisions and
Listing~\ref{lst:reproduce} make the reported counts in
Section~\ref{sec:capability_assessment} repeatable, but mechanical checks
verify totals and identifiers rather than the adequacy of classifications.
This is why Table~\ref{tab:coverage} reports exercised mechanisms separately
from the design requirements assessed in
Tables~\ref{tab:olr_coverage}--\ref{tab:sg_coverage}. Neither a large test
suite nor high coverage establishes institutional assurance. Tests exposing
gaps have the same evidential standing as tests of successful operations.

\paragraph{Answers to the Evaluation Questions.}
\emph{EQ1:} The baseline maps 36 requirements to 2 E, 16 P, and 18 N classifications. Supplemental evidence supports selected clauses without changing that mapping.
\emph{EQ2:} Tests distinguish selected admitted operations from violations and expose bypasses and missing safeguards. The supplemental profile rejects additional decision, validation, content, and receipt violations within its scope. The model-linked prototype extends this feasibility evidence to selected model-derived parameters, mandate checks, and admission guards.
\emph{EQ3:} Pinned packages provide repeatable execution and measured coverage. The separate modeling checks establish schema compatibility, graph preservation, and mechanical traceability. The feasibility package adds repeatable runtime traces for selected model bindings. Independent replication and complete requirement satisfaction are not established.
\emph{EQ4:} The baseline lacks verified decision-to-publication binding. The supplemental episode demonstrates a bounded local chain, not atomic compound transitions or the complete gate in Equation~\ref{eq:institutional_confirmation}. None of the implementation evaluations establishes legal adequacy, independent institutional participation, or production suitability.

The remaining issues are decision-to-result evidence across services, effective distribution of authority, and valid mandates and review procedures. Section~\ref{sec:conclusions} develops the corresponding priorities.

\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

This study develops an institutional specialization of DAOMod
for knowledge graph lifecycle governance. The design connects
versioned semantic assets, organizational authority, collective
decisions, execution evidence, and institutional safeguards.
Its 36 requirements connect to an integrated ontology,
editable worked models, and proposed notation, as well as a
separate assessment of implementation support.

\paragraph{Answers to the Research Questions.}

\emph{RQ1.}
The DAO-ML Organization layer requires asset and version
references, lifecycle states and transition types, and
permissions scoped jointly by role or committee, asset,
and transition. OLR1--OLR12 also specify evidence categories,
governed organizational changes, coordination-policy references,
and distribution constraints. These elements establish
who is eligible to act and within what scope, without
treating eligibility as a completed authorization decision.

\emph{RQ2.}
ET-DM coordination and execution constructs require explicit
connections between proposals, decisions, authorizations,
operations, and results. CR1--CR6 specify transition plans,
evidence, participation, policies, and decision outcomes.
ER1--ER6 specify scoped authorization, execution preconditions,
content identification, attributable receipts, and technical
confirmation. Compound transitions additionally require
evidence that all authorized changes take effect together.

\emph{RQ3.}
SG1--SG12 qualify technical execution through accountable
mandates, governance lineage, forward-only correction,
bounded exceptional powers, compliance references, protected
evidence, and controls for capture and operational failure.
Institutional confirmation requires both a matching technical
result and satisfaction of the applicable modeled safeguards.
It does not establish the legal validity of the underlying
policy or action.

\emph{MRQ.}
DAOMod can be specialized by organizing authority around
versioned semantic-asset transitions and connecting that
authority to collective decisions, exact operations,
observed results, and institutional checks.
Sections~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}--\ref{sec:safeguards}
provide this design specification. Its institutional
effectiveness remains conditional on implementation,
valid mandates, and evaluation in the intended setting.

\paragraph{Contribution and Evidence.}

The contribution is the specialization, its integrated ontology,
worked models, proposed notation, and explicit requirement
mappings. Appendices~\ref{app:dao_ml_notation}--\ref{app:woodhive_models}
make the notation and worked applications inspectable.
It separates organizational eligibility,
collective acceptance, executable authorization, technical
confirmation, and institutional confirmation. These distinctions
allow a reviewer to identify both the controls required
by the design and the evidence supporting their implementation.
Model checks confirm schema compatibility, graph preservation,
and resolvable bindings. They do not prove full OWL consistency,
complete semantic adequacy, or effective institutional use.

Section~\ref{sec:evaluation} demonstrates selected contract-level
mechanisms and identifies substantial implementation gaps.
The supplemental episode adds exact committee binding,
canonical content verification, and receipt-based confirmation
for one local publication profile. The model-linked proof of concept
further shows that selected ontology/model bindings can inform an
executable configuration and remain traceable to its result. It does not demonstrate
atomic compound transitions or the complete institutional
safeguard gate. The findings therefore support bounded
feasibility rather than complete lifecycle governance
or institutional suitability.

\paragraph{Future Work.}

Three priorities follow from the limitations in
Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

First, the local publication episode needs to become a complete hybrid
lifecycle before the design can support the full range of governed changes.
That extension requires explicit admission and execution records, visible
failure and timeout outcomes, and escalation when receipts are missing.
Replacement, deprecation, restoration, and archival must then use those
controls. For compound operations, a common commit or equivalent mechanism
must make the changes visible together, while the institutional confirmation
conditions determine when a result becomes authoritative.

Second, authority must be evaluated across independent institutions,
because the local tests cannot show how divergent interests affect shared
control. Studies should examine whether scoped permissions, incompatible
assignments, and delegation limits preserve the intended decision rights
under realistic workloads. Committee composition, quorum, and concentration
thresholds must be assessed together with participation and obstruction,
including capture, deadlock, exceptional-power use, and selective non-execution.

Third, obtain independent technical and institutional review
of the requirements, evidence mappings, and safeguard policies.
A second operator should reproduce the contract episodes and
model checks, and modelers should assess notation clarity and
usability. Jurisdiction-specific assessment should examine mandates,
retention, disclosure, appeals, and emergency procedures.
The materials in Section~\ref{sec:artifact_availability}
support reproduction and review, but completed independent

\section{DAO-ML Institutional Visual Notation}
\label{app:dao_ml_notation}

This appendix defines version 1.1.0 of the proposed visual
notation for the Organization-layer additions. It supplements the DAO-ML
constructs in Section~\ref{sec:governance_requirements}; it does
not replace the upstream language or claim editor compatibility.
Appendix~\ref{app:etdm_notation} defines the Coordination and
Execution additions, and Appendix~\ref{app:woodhive_models}
applies both notations to WoodHive.

\paragraph{Node Grammar.}
A reader must be able to distinguish an institutional addition from an
upstream symbol before interpreting its meaning. Each new node therefore
uses a card frame with two protruding right-edge tabs and a reserved
\texttt{IKG} family marker. Its layer badge locates the concept in
Organization (O), Coordination (C), Execution (E), or Safeguard (S).

Within that frame, an exact ontology-class stereotype distinguishes
concepts that share a family. For example, \texttt{ScopedPermission}
and \texttt{AuthorityScope} both use SCOPE without becoming the same class.
A stable instance identifier, requirement identifier, and concrete fields
or resolvable worked-instance pointer connect the symbol to the modeled
case. Color is optional and adds no independent meaning, so these labels
remain necessary when a diagram is viewed without color.

Table~\ref{tab:notation_families} shows the shared families.
The marker is part of a labeled compound symbol, not a standalone
class identifier. The nine markers are distinct. The paired tabs,
reserved prefix, family code, and exact stereotype identify an
institutional extension; a plain geometric shape is not assigned
a new meaning. This avoids overloading the upstream symbols.

The notation is checked against Tables~4.1--4.3, 4.5--4.6, and
4.8 of Avanzo's thesis \cite{Avanzo2025Thesis}. In particular,
committee ellipses, Treasury hexagons, goal parallelograms,
Proposal corner cuts, role/agent icons, transaction lambda
symbols, data-store cylinders, and on-chain/snapshot wrappers
retain their original meanings. The first-draft capsule and
hexagon markers are replaced because they could be confused
with those symbols. This comparison concerns the thesis's core
notation tables, not universal visual novelty or a usability study.

\paragraph{Required Organizational Meaning.}
The Organization symbols distinguish the object being governed from
the authority to change it. An asset symbol identifies the resource and
immutable versions, while its lifecycle symbol records permitted states
and transitions separately from collective approval. A scoped permission
then connects a subject to an asset or asset class, transition, and action.
Functional roles and collective bodies retain references to the upstream
model elements; the new frames expose institutional bindings rather than
redefine the original glyphs.

Assignment and distribution constraints make the limits of that authority
visible, including incompatible responsibilities, bounded delegation,
accountable principals, and the prohibition on sole end-to-end control.
Policy and evidence links identify what must be considered without
turning those references into authorization by themselves.

\paragraph{Connection Rules.}
New directed extension edges use tandem arrowheads and a
reserved \texttt{IKG:} label. An ontology edge carries its exact
declared property name after that prefix and respects its
domain and range. A dashed
\texttt{IKG:baseRef} edge resolves to an upstream model element.
A dashed \texttt{IKG:field:<key>} edge identifies a reference
actually encoded in a worked value; it is not an undeclared
ontology property. A dotted requirement link identifies
traceability. Process-order edges belong to a separate trace
view and use \texttt{IKG:next [guard]}; they do not imply that an
operation is implemented or atomic. A reference to a mandate
or constraint does not by itself establish its validity or
satisfaction.

\paragraph{Coordination and Execution Meaning.}
The Coordination symbols explain what is proposed and how it can be
accepted. A plan identifies every step, exact version, expected source
and target state, and supporting evidence; admission connects participation
to current eligibility and mandates. The decision policy supplies the
mechanism, quorum, threshold, timing, and delegation conditions.
Keeping terminal outcomes separate from challenged or suspended process
statuses prevents a paused proposal from being mistaken for a final decision.

The Execution symbols carry the accepted scope into an operation.
Authorization records the decision, complete plan, appointed executor,
expiry, and nonce; it does not become an asset lifecycle state.
Execution guards identify preconditions, and content-identity records
specify how canonicalization and hashing establish the expected content.
The receipt then reports the attempt, status, failure information, result
manifest, and common commit where required. Confirmation references that
receipt and its idempotence key so a reported result can be checked without
duplicating the lifecycle effect.

The ontology links are \texttt{hasAsset} from plan to asset,
\texttt{requiresPolicy} from plan to coordination policy,
\texttt{hasPlan} from authorization to plan,
\texttt{hasReceipt} from confirmation to receipt, and
\texttt{hasMandate} from admission to mandate. The \texttt{IKG:} visual prefix does not change the property URI.
Tandem arrowheads point
from the property subject to its object; they are not a
chronological execution sequence.

\paragraph{Safeguard Interfaces.}
S-badged symbols expose the institutional conditions that technical
confirmation alone cannot establish. Mandates, lineage, and rationale
make authority and responsibility inspectable; compliance references,
protected-evidence policies, and external decisions state obligations
that can constrain execution. Separation, monitoring, and escalation
provide a response to concentrated authority or failure.

The exceptional-action symbols make the same limits explicit during
recovery. Emergency authority displays its operation scope, trigger,
duration, delegation rules, notification, and independent review.
Restoration requires fresh authorization for a complete plan that preserves
earlier history. Neither a technical confirmation nor a reference to these
safeguards silently establishes legal approval or satisfaction of the
institutional conditions.

\paragraph{Notation Status.}
The catalog, view schema, editable legend, worked diagrams,
and validation scripts are supplied with the model package in
Section~\ref{sec:model_artifact_validation}. Catalog and
binding checks establish mechanical consistency, not
perceptual clarity, usability, semantic completeness, or
independent scholarly acceptance.

\paragraph{Working Through the Models.}
First, identify the asset and version, its lifecycle transition,
eligible roles, scoped permissions, and distribution constraint
in the Organization view. Second, create the exact transition
plan and connect its evidence, admission conditions, and
coordination policy. Third, record the decision and bind any
accepted authorization to the plan, executor, validity period,
and nonce. Fourth, specify the execution attempt, content
comparison, result receipt, technical confirmation, and
applicable institutional conditions. A failed or missing
receipt follows an explicit failure and escalation path.

For restoration, replace the single publication step with
a complete plan containing retained-v1 reactivation and
current-v2 supersession. Require fresh authorization and a
common commit while preserving prior history. The compound
plan is a design obligation; the model example does not
establish an implemented transactional graph store.

A modeler edits the authoritative instance and profile,
regenerates the views, and runs the schema, ontology, binding,
and notation checks described in
Section~\ref{sec:model_artifact_validation}. The separate
validators assess representation consistency. Human review
must still assess whether the chosen scopes, policies,
responsibilities, and safeguards are adequate for the
institutional setting.
